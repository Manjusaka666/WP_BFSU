\section{Identification Strategy}\label{sec:identification}

\subsection{Identification challenge}

The core test regresses forecast errors on expectation revisions. A negative coefficient discriminates diagnostic expectations from both rational expectations and information rigidity. The identification challenge is that common macro shocks can move revisions and errors simultaneously: a food-price spike may shift expectations upward while independently depressing realized inflation through a supply channel, generating a spurious negative correlation. With 32 quarterly observations, conventional control strategies risk overfitting, sharpening the need for a parsimonious yet discriminating approach.

\subsection{Primary strategy: OLS revision-error test}

The revision-error regression provides the main identification. Under diagnostic expectations, households overweight salient signals when revising beliefs, so upward revisions systematically overshoot and produce negative subsequent forecast errors. The testable prediction is $\beta_1 < 0$ in
\[
  FE_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \Delta\mu_t + B' W_t + \varepsilon_t,
\]
where $FE_{t+1}$ is the next-quarter forecast error, $\Delta\mu_t$ is the expectation revision, and $W_t$ contains macro controls (food CPI, M2 growth, PPI inflation).

This coefficient cleanly discriminates among three leading models. Under rational expectations, revisions are orthogonal to subsequent errors, so $\beta_1 = 0$. Under information rigidity without overreaction, revisions are too small and subsequent errors reflect residual adjustment, implying $\beta_1 > 0$. Only diagnostic expectations predict $\beta_1 < 0$: the revision overshoots the information content of the signal, and the overshoot reverses in the next period.

Inference uses Newey--West standard errors with a Bartlett kernel to account for serial correlation and heteroskedasticity. The OLS test does not require an instrument, so it avoids weak-instrument distortion and delivers the sharpest discriminating evidence in this short sample.

\subsection{Supplementary evidence: salience-IV design}

As supplementary evidence, I construct an instrumental-variables design that isolates variation in revisions originating from attention reallocation rather than from direct inflation determinants. The IV provides directional support for the diagnostic mechanism but, as documented below, the weak first stage limits what 2SLS alone can establish.

\paragraph{Media Salience Index (MSI).}
The salience index combines two components: a standardized measure of economic-policy uncertainty (EPU) capturing the intensity of economic news in domestic media, and a survey-based measure of the fraction of respondents expressing directional expectations (as opposed to uncertainty), which proxies for household engagement with price information. The index is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:msi}
  MSI_t = 0.6 \cdot z(EPU_t) + 0.4 \cdot z(\text{ExpIndex}_t),
\end{equation}
where $z(\cdot)$ denotes standardization to zero mean and unit variance. The weights reflect the relative importance of supply-side (media-generated) versus demand-side (household-reported) attention; the diagnostic coefficient is not sensitive to moderate weight variations (Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

\paragraph{Media Congestion Instrument.}
The instrument is the negative standardized Geopolitical Risk index:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:iv}
  Z_t^{cong} = -z(GPR_t).
\end{equation}
Geopolitical events (territorial disputes, military threats, trade-war escalation) crowd media attention toward non-economic news, reducing the bandwidth available for inflation coverage. When GPR is high, media capacity is absorbed by geopolitical reporting, and inflation signals receive less household attention even if actual price pressures are unchanged. The negative sign ensures the instrument is positively related to media congestion. This attention-reallocation mechanism is independent of domestic inflation determinants as long as geopolitical events do not simultaneously cause price changes through commodity or exchange-rate channels---a threat evaluated explicitly below.

\subsection{Estimating equations}

The three-equation chain traces the path from instrument to outcome:
\begin{align}
  S_t &= \pi_0 + \pi_1 Z_t^{cong} + \Pi' W_t + u_t,
    \label{eq:first_stage} \\
  \Delta\mu_t &= \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 \hat{S}_t + A' W_t + e_t,
    \label{eq:second_stage_revision} \\
  FE_{t+1} &= \beta_0 + \beta_1 \widehat{\Delta\mu}_t + B' W_t
    + \varepsilon_t, \label{eq:second_stage_fe}
\end{align}
where $S_t$ is salience, $\Delta\mu_t$ is the expectation revision, $FE_{t+1}$ is the next-quarter forecast error, $W_t$ is a vector of macro controls, and hats denote fitted values from the preceding stage.

The diagnostic prediction remains $\beta_1 < 0$. I report weak-instrument-robust confidence intervals from the Anderson--Rubin procedure, which remains valid regardless of first-stage strength and does not require asymptotic normality of the 2SLS estimator.

\subsection{First-stage strength}

Table~\ref{tab:iv_first_stage} reports first-stage results. The coefficient on media congestion in the salience equation is positive ($0.082$, SE $= 0.076$, $p = 0.29$): a one-standard-deviation decrease in GPR increases salience, though the relationship is imprecise. The first-stage Wald F-statistic is $1.16$, well below the \citet{Stock2005} threshold of $10$. The weak first stage prevents informative 2SLS, making the reduced form the operative IV evidence. This weakness motivates both the Anderson--Rubin inference and the reduced-form strategy that bypasses the first stage entirely; the OLS diagnostic test, which does not rely on instrument strength, remains the primary discriminating evidence.

Figure~\ref{fig:first_stage_iv} plots the first-stage relationship. The association is economically interpretable: quarters where geopolitical events consumed media attention (2016Q1 South China Sea tensions, 2018Q3 trade-war escalation, 2020Q1 pandemic onset, 2022Q1 Russia--Ukraine conflict) correspond to low inflation salience, while calmer periods leave more media space for economic coverage.

\subsection{Threats to exclusion and falsification}

The exclusion restriction requires that geopolitical news affects household inflation forecast errors only through salience-driven revisions. Four threats merit explicit evaluation.

\paragraph{Threat 1: Commodity-price channels.}
Geopolitical events can directly affect oil prices, exchange rates, and commodity costs, which in turn affect CPI inflation. If GPR shocks move both salience and realized inflation, the instrument violates exclusion. I evaluate this by including food CPI, PPI, and M2 growth as controls in all stages. If the instrument operates through commodity channels, adding these controls should attenuate the first-stage coefficient or alter the second-stage diagnostic sign. Neither occurs: the diagnostic coefficient is stable across nested control sets (Table~\ref{tab:ols_robustness}).

\paragraph{Threat 2: Broad macro shocks.}
Geopolitical events may cause generalized economic disruption that moves all expectations simultaneously, capturing a recession or uncertainty effect rather than attention reallocation. The placebo test estimates the same IV chain with non-inflation forecast errors (industrial output surprise, employment gap) as dependent variables. If the instrument picks up broad macro shocks, these placebo outcomes should show comparable coefficients. Table~\ref{tab:placebo_tests} reports that placebo effects are small relative to the main effect and statistically indistinguishable from zero.

\paragraph{Threat 3: Anticipation.}
If households anticipate geopolitical events and adjust expectations in advance, the timing structure is contaminated. The anticipation test regresses current forecast errors on lead (future) revisions: if anticipation is present, tomorrow's revision should predict today's forecast error. The lead coefficient is null (Table~\ref{tab:placebo_tests}, column~3), supporting the timing assumption.

\paragraph{Threat 4: Finite-sample fragility.}
With 32 observations, a single influential quarter could drive the result. The permutation test randomly reassigns the instrument across quarters 300 times and re-estimates the diagnostic coefficient. The permutation distribution has a mean of $14.8$ with a 95\% range of $[-129.7, 76.9]$ (Table~\ref{tab:placebo_tests}), which includes the estimated coefficient. The wide permutation distribution reflects 2SLS imprecision given the weak first stage, not evidence against the diagnostic hypothesis. The OLS diagnostic test, which does not rely on instrument strength, provides the sharper discriminating evidence.
