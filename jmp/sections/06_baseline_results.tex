\section{Baseline Results}\label{sec:results}

Three results together support the diagnostic expectations hypothesis. The CFPS household panel ($N \approx 13{,}700$, $\hat{\beta} = -0.55$, $p < 0.001$) is the primary finding: it delivers credible within-variation identification via province and wave fixed effects and is far more precisely estimated than any aggregate result. The aggregate OLS ($\hat{\beta} = -2.24$, $p = 0.055$, $N = 32$) is secondary corroboration: the key test is sign consistency with the household result, not the $p$-value alone. The IV directional check reinforces both but cannot pin down magnitudes, as detailed in Section~\ref{subsec:iv}.

\subsection{OLS estimates}\label{subsec:ols}

Table~\ref{tab:ols_baseline} reports the aggregate diagnostic test:
regressing next-quarter forecast errors on forecast revisions across
nested specifications. The diagnostic prediction is
$\beta < 0$---positive revisions should predict negative subsequent
errors if forecasters overreact to new information.

The bivariate specification yields $\hat{\beta} = -1.66$
(SE $= 0.96$, $p = 0.093$). Adding macro controls (food CPI, M2,
PPI) produces $\hat{\beta} = -2.24$
(SE $= 1.11$, $p = 0.055$). The full specification with uncertainty
controls (EPU, GPR) yields $\hat{\beta} = -2.13$
(SE $= 1.26$, $p = 0.103$, $N = 32$). Both $p = 0.055$ and
$p = 0.103$ are marginal by conventional thresholds. The relevant
test is whether the sign is negative, as the diagnostic model
predicts; all three specifications pass it. Aggregate power is
limited to 32 observations, and the CFPS panel is the vehicle for
precise estimation.

The economic magnitude is the more informative summary. The
preferred estimate $\hat{\beta} = -2.24$ implies that a
1~percentage-point upward revision predicts a 2.24~pp subsequent
forecast error in the opposite direction. A one-standard-deviation
increase in the revision ($\sigma_{FR} = 0.45$~pp) shifts the
forecast error by approximately $-1.0$~pp.
That shift is large relative to the signed mean forecast error
($\bar{FE}=1.32$~pp, not MAE) in Table~\ref{tab:desc_stats}
in the aggregate sample. The $R^2$ rises from 0.048 (bivariate) to
0.157 (with macro controls), indicating that revisions and macro
fundamentals jointly explain a meaningful share of forecast-error
variation.

Coefficient stability across control sets provides further evidence
against single-omitted-variable explanations.
Dropping individual controls (Table~\ref{tab:ols_robustness})
produces estimates ranging from $-1.71$ to $-2.11$, all retaining
the diagnostic sign. Controlling for food CPI does not eliminate
the revision--error link; the coefficient strengthens, suggesting a
diagnostic mechanism that operates through attention to food prices
but is not mechanically caused by food-price mean reversion alone.

Column~(4) of Table~\ref{tab:ols_baseline} reports a specification
using an alternative revision measure and yields $\hat{\beta} = 0.535$
($p < 0.01$, $N = 36$). This column replaces the Carlson--Parkin
forecast revision ($FR_t = \mu^{CP}_t - \mu^{CP}_{t-1}$) with the
quarter-on-quarter change in the PBoC's raw inflation expectation
diffusion index ($FR^{idx}_t = I_t - I_{t-1}$, where $I_t$ is the
proportion-weighted index ranging from roughly 57 to 68 with
$\text{SD} \approx 3.4$). The positive coefficient does not
contradict the diagnostic finding for two reasons.
First, the raw diffusion index measures the \emph{balance of opinion}
(share expecting price increases minus decreases), not a quantified
inflation rate; its revision captures shifts in the categorical
distribution rather than changes in a point forecast, so the
regression conflates the scale of the dependent variable
(forecast error in percentage points) with an index-point regressor.
Second, a rising diffusion index signals broadening inflation
concern, which typically coincides with periods when actual inflation
also accelerates; the positive coefficient therefore reflects the
co-movement of sentiment breadth and realized inflation rather than
overreaction to new information. The three specifications based on
the Carlson--Parkin quantification, which maps survey responses onto
a common percentage-point scale with the dependent variable, provide
the appropriate test of diagnostic expectations.

Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap_inference} reports finite-sample-robust
inference for the preferred specification. I implement a wild bootstrap
with Rademacher weights (5{,}000 replications). The bootstrap
$t$-statistic test rejects $H_0\colon \beta=0$ at the 5\% level
($p = 0.030$). The 95\% percentile confidence interval, however, is
$[-2.52, 2.42]$ and crosses zero, so the percentile method does not
reject at 5\%. This divergence is expected in small samples: the
$t$-statistic is asymptotically pivotal, giving it better finite-sample
size control, whereas the percentile interval is not pivotal and can be
conservative when the sampling distribution is skewed or wide.
I follow the $t$-test result as the more reliable of the two.
A note on standard errors: the OLS baseline table reports Newey--West
standard errors with four fixed lags ($\text{SE} = 1.114$), while the
bootstrap table reports HAC standard errors with automatic bandwidth
selection ($\text{SE} = 1.023$). Both are consistent; the bootstrap
uses the same automatic bandwidth as its underlying $t$-statistic.
Randomization inference, which permutes the revision series 5{,}000
times and re-estimates the coefficient, produces a one-sided $p$-value
of 0.060. This procedure is valid regardless of the error distribution
and does not rely on large-sample approximations. Taken together, every
method points to a negative revision coefficient. The sign is robust
across all inference procedures, but the magnitude remains imprecisely
estimated in 32 observations.

\subsection{IV estimates}\label{subsec:iv}

The salience-IV design is a directional check, not an independent
identification strategy. The first stage is weak: the Wald
F-statistic is $1.16$ (Stock--Yogo threshold: 10), and the
instrument coefficient is $0.082$ (SE $= 0.076$, $p = 0.291$).
The 2SLS point estimate ($\hat{\beta}_1^{IV} = 33.7$,
SE $= 108.6$) is numerically uninformative.

The Anderson--Rubin 95\% confidence interval is $[-3.0, 3.0]$.
This interval is weak-instrument-robust but wide: it spans the
OLS estimates and excludes large positive values that would
indicate underreaction, yet it is too diffuse to pin down the
diagnostic coefficient. IV inference does not establish causality
here; the paper does not claim it does.

The reduced-form coefficient---the congestion instrument regressed
directly on forecast errors---is $-0.41$ (SE $= 0.49$,
$p = 0.41$). The negative sign is consistent with the diagnostic
prediction: higher media congestion (lower inflation salience)
associates with smaller subsequent errors. The sign reinforces
the aggregate OLS and the CFPS panel results. The lack of
precision in 32 observations is expected given the weak first
stage, and limits what this exercise can add beyond directional
confirmation.

\subsection{Local projections}\label{subsec:lp}

I estimate local projections following \citet{Jorda2005} to
trace how the revision--error relationship evolves over time:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lp}
  y_{t+h} = \alpha_h + \beta_h FR_t + \Gamma_h' W_t
    + \varepsilon_{t+h}, \qquad h = 0, 1, \ldots, 8,
\end{equation}
where $y_{t+h}$ is either the forecast error or realized
inflation at horizon $h$, and $FR_t$ is the revision at time $t$.

Table~\ref{tab:lp_dynamic} and
Figures~\ref{fig:lp_fe}--\ref{fig:lp_pi} report the results.
The forecast-error response is negative on impact
($\hat{\beta}_0 = -2.24$, SE $= 1.11$), consistent with the
baseline OLS estimate. It oscillates at longer horizons, with
negative values at $h = 2$ ($-1.37$) and $h = 4$ ($-2.25$,
significant) and positive values at $h = 1$ and $h = 3$. The
oscillation reflects the short sample ($N$ falls from 32 at
$h = 0$ to 23 at $h = 8$) interacting with seasonal patterns
in Chinese CPI.

The realized-inflation response is positive and significant at
impact ($\hat{\beta}_0 = 3.64$, SE $= 1.03$), confirming that
revisions partly capture genuine inflationary news. Yet the
forecast-error response exceeds the inflation response in
absolute value at $h = 0$, and the inflation response reverses
by $h = 1$ ($-1.76$). This pattern is consistent with
overreaction layered on rational updating: forecasters respond
to real news but overshoot. The LP evidence confirms the
negative contemporaneous sign and shows that the revision--error
link is not an artifact of longer-horizon confounding.
