\section{Baseline Results}\label{sec:results}

This section presents a strict evidence hierarchy. The CFPS household
panel is the primary result because it provides the most precise estimate
under the strongest design in this paper
($\hat{\beta} \approx -0.55$, $p < 0.001$, $N \approx 13{,}700$). The
PBoC aggregate series is corroborating evidence: it keeps the same sign
and meaningful magnitude in a low-power quarterly sample
($\hat{\beta} = -2.24$, $p = 0.055$, $N = 32$). The IV exercise is
reported only as directional support because first-stage strength is weak
($F = 1.16$), so IV cannot discipline magnitudes. The ordering is
intentional: micro evidence anchors identification, aggregate evidence
checks external consistency, and IV is interpretive rather than
determinative.

\subsection{CFPS Household Panel (Main Result)}
\label{subsec:cfps_main_result}

The central estimate of the paper is the household-level revision--error
slope in the CFPS panel. A one-unit upward revision in inflation
expectations predicts a subsequent forecast error that is
0.55 units lower, holding province and wave effects fixed. In magnitude,
this implies partial reversal of the revision itself: households that
revise upward the most are exactly those that later overpredict inflation
the most. The estimate is sharp ($p < 0.001$) and based on roughly
13,700 household-wave observations, which is an order-of-magnitude
increase in usable variation relative to the aggregate time series.

The identifying variation is within province and within survey wave.
Province fixed effects absorb time-invariant regional inflation
environments. Wave fixed effects absorb common national shocks, including
food and energy episodes, policy communication, and broad macro
conditions. What remains is cross-household variation in revision
behavior inside the same province-wave cell. Under that design, the
negative slope is difficult to rationalize as a mechanical time-series
artifact.

Economic magnitude comes first. The estimate
$\hat{\beta} \approx -0.55$ means that when two otherwise comparable
households differ by one unit in revision direction/intensity, the
household with the larger upward revision makes a forecast error about
half a unit more negative in the next realization window. In this
context, the effect is not a small residual correction; it is a first-
order component of expectation mistakes. Put differently, the response to
new inflation information appears too strong, and roughly one-half of the
revision is undone in the subsequent error.

The pattern is also stable in a progressive-control sense. A parsimonious
specification with only revisions already delivers a negative slope.
Adding household demographics and then province and wave fixed effects
does not attenuate the coefficient in economically meaningful ways. In
the Oster bounds table, the uncontrolled estimate
($\hat{\beta} = -0.5199$) and the controlled estimate
($\hat{\beta} = -0.5238$) are nearly identical, despite the larger
covariate set and fixed effects. Richer controls improve
credibility without flipping sign, collapsing magnitude, or relying on a
single fragile covariate.

This progression can be read in the same nested-column language used in
progressive-controls empirical tables. Column~(1) isolates the raw revision--error
covariance. Column~(2) adds household observables that capture life-cycle
and socioeconomic differences in inflation perception. Column~(3) adds
province and wave fixed effects, forcing the estimate to be identified
only by within-cell contrasts. The coefficient remains tightly centered
near $-0.55$ through this sequence. In other words, the estimate does not
appear because controls were omitted; it survives when controls are
stacked in the order most likely to absorb confounding.

The robustness logic here is not ``everything is significant.'' It is
that magnitude, sign, and design move together in the expected direction.
First, precision is high because the panel contributes many more
effective observations than the aggregate sample. Second, the sign is
stable across model enrichment. Third, inferential threats from omitted
variables would need implausibly large selection on unobservables to
drive the coefficient to zero. This combination makes the CFPS slope the
main empirical object for interpretation.

The interpretation remains conditional. The estimate is a reduced-form
diagnostic slope in expectation errors, not a structural parameter pinned
down by exogenous treatment timing. But conditional on the fixed-effects
design and observed controls, the evidence supports the overreaction
mechanism more strongly than either rational expectations
($\beta = 0$) or canonical information-rigidity dynamics
($\beta > 0$ in this setup). The paper's core quantitative claim should
therefore be stated at the household level: revisions and subsequent
errors move in opposite directions with economically large elasticity.

Heterogeneity results in Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity} reinforce this
reading rather than weaken it. Interactions by education, income, and
urban status leave the base revision slope near $-0.55$ in every case,
with no strong differential slopes. The lack of sharp interaction effects
is informative: overreaction is broad-based, not confined to a narrow
subgroup. For a macro application, this matters because aggregate
implications require behavior that generalizes beyond a single
demographic tail.

The CFPS result also has a practical interpretation for policy models.
Suppose a policy communication shock shifts expected inflation upward by
one unit in survey space. The estimated slope implies that roughly
one-half of that adjustment is offset by subsequent forecast error in the
opposite direction. That degree of overshooting is large enough to matter
for short-horizon policy calibration: mechanical incorporation of raw
household revisions into inflation nowcasts would overstate persistent
pressure unless one explicitly adjusts for diagnostic reversal.

Finally, the sample-size contrast is substantive, not cosmetic.
Aggregate evidence with $N = 32$ can validate sign consistency but cannot
deliver the same precision frontier as household microdata. Treating
CFPS as primary and aggregate OLS as corroborating is therefore a design
choice rooted in statistical content, not narrative preference.

\subsection{Aggregate Corroboration}
\label{subsec:aggregate_corroboration}

The aggregate exercise asks whether the micro result survives temporal
aggregation and market-wide conditioning information. The preferred
specification yields $\hat{\beta} = -2.24$ with $N = 32$
($p = 0.055$). Magnitude first: a 1 percentage-point upward revision in
aggregate expected inflation predicts a 2.24 percentage-point forecast
error in the opposite direction one quarter ahead. Even with marginal
classical significance, this is economically large and aligned with the
CFPS sign.

The progression across specifications in Table~\ref{tab:ols_baseline} is
informative. The bivariate slope is already negative
($-1.66$). Adding macro controls (food CPI, M2, PPI) strengthens the
magnitude to $-2.24$. Adding uncertainty controls (EPU, GPR) keeps the
sign and preserves a large negative point estimate ($-2.13$). In short,
conditioning choices move precision but do not reverse the core slope.
Given the small quarterly sample, sign stability across nested columns is
the key model-discrimination object.

Finite-sample inference confirms that the negative slope is not an
artifact of a single asymptotic approximation. The bootstrap
$t$-test yields $p = 0.030$, stronger than the baseline HAC
asymptotic $p$-value. The percentile confidence interval is wider and
includes zero, which is expected in short samples with skewed finite-
sample distributions. The correct interpretation is therefore not
``conclusive rejection in every metric''; it is that direction and
economic size are stable, while precision remains bounded by $N = 32$.

Magnitude comparisons help anchor the aggregate estimate. Using the
reported revision dispersion in the aggregate sample, a typical
one-standard-deviation revision movement implies roughly a one-
percentage-point opposite-signed forecast-error movement under the
baseline slope. Relative to average signed forecast errors in the sample,
that is not a second-order adjustment. It is large enough to affect
quarter-ahead narrative interpretation in real time, especially when
policy discussions rely on survey revisions as a leading signal.

Column~(4) in Table~\ref{tab:ols_baseline} uses the alternative PBoC
diffusion-index revision measure and returns a positive slope. That
column should be read as a measurement comparison, not a contradiction of
the baseline result. The diffusion index is a balance-of-opinion
indicator, whereas the baseline dependent variable is in percentage-point
forecast-error units under Carlson--Parkin quantification. A positive
coefficient in that mixed-scale specification can reflect co-movement
between broadening inflation concern and realized inflation, rather than
the overreaction mapping tested by the baseline scale-consistent columns.
For hypothesis testing in this section, the baseline quantified-revision
specifications remain the relevant reference.

Taken together, the aggregate block corroborates the CFPS evidence in the
dimension that matters most for model comparison: sign and meaningful
magnitude. It does not independently settle causal interpretation, and it
is not used that way. Its role is to show that the household-level
revision--error mapping survives when the data are collapsed to the same
quarterly objects used in policy communication.

This corroboration role is important for external validity. Household
micro evidence could, in principle, reflect questionnaire-specific
mechanics or individual-level coding noise. The aggregate exercise
addresses that concern by showing that the same directional mapping
appears in the quarterly macro environment where central-bank surveys and
market commentary are interpreted. The estimate is less precise at that
level, but it is directionally coherent with the micro result and
economically material in size.

Additional aggregate diagnostics, control sensitivity tables, and
correlation matrices are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness} so the
main text keeps the identification hierarchy transparent.

\subsection{Dynamic Response}
\label{subsec:dynamic_response}

Static coefficients summarize average predictive content, but they do not
show horizon-by-horizon adjustment. I therefore estimate local
projections for horizons $h = 0,1,\ldots,8$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lp}
  y_{t+h} = \alpha_h + \beta_h FR_t + \Gamma_h' W_t + \varepsilon_{t+h},
\end{equation}
where $y_{t+h}$ is either forecast error or realized inflation. The
impact coefficient is negative for forecast errors, then oscillates
across horizons in the short sample.

On impact, the forecast-error response is
$\hat{\beta}_0 = -2.24$ (SE $= 1.11$), matching the baseline OLS
magnitude by construction. At longer horizons, the path alternates in
sign, with negative responses at selected even horizons and positive
responses at adjacent odd horizons. Two features matter for
interpretation. First, the impact sign is unambiguously negative. Second,
the oscillation arrives as the effective sample shrinks with horizon
($N$ declines from 32 at $h=0$ to 23 at $h=8$), so medium-horizon
coefficients should be read as suggestive dynamics rather than precise
long-run elasticities.

The inflation-response LP complements this pattern. Realized inflation
reacts positively on impact, consistent with revisions containing genuine
news, but the forecast-error response is still strongly negative at
impact. That combination is what one expects under ``news plus
overreaction'': agents update in the right direction yet overshoot in
magnitude, creating opposite-signed forecast errors afterward. The
subsequent oscillation is consistent with short-horizon correction rather
than persistent one-sided bias.

In magnitude terms, the LP block strengthens the baseline message. The
impact effect is not only negative but large relative to typical quarter-
to-quarter forecast-error movements in this sample. The dynamic profiles
also show that the effect is front-loaded: most economically relevant
action occurs immediately, and later coefficients fluctuate around a much
less stable trajectory. This temporal shape is consistent with salient signals triggering rapid overweighting followed by partial
correction.

The dynamic evidence also clarifies what the baseline coefficient is and
is not. It is not a long-horizon persistence parameter. Instead, it is a
short-horizon response coefficient concentrated near impact. Once this
timing pattern is explicit, the apparent tension between large impact
slopes and weaker medium-horizon precision disappears: they describe
different parts of the adjustment path. For policy interpretation, that
means revision shocks should be treated as near-term overshooting signals,
not as durable medium-run drift in expectations errors.

\subsection{IV Directional Check}
\label{subsec:iv}

The IV exercise is intentionally brief in the main text because its role
is narrow. First-stage strength is weak ($F = 1.16$), so 2SLS magnitudes
are not reliable for structural interpretation in this sample. The
Anderson--Rubin 95\% confidence set is wide ($[-3,3]$), and the reduced-
form coefficient is negative ($-0.41$), matching the direction of the
CFPS and aggregate OLS results.

These facts support one statement and no more: IV evidence is
directionally consistent with diagnostic overreaction, but it cannot rank
plausible magnitudes. That is why the paper's quantitative conclusions
rest on CFPS plus aggregate corroboration, not on IV point estimates.
Detailed IV implementation and exclusion diagnostics remain in
Section~\ref{sec:identification} and Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}.

This placement is deliberate for editorial clarity. Keeping weak-IV
output in a concise final subsection prevents the main results section
from overstating what the instrument can deliver while still documenting
that the reduced-form direction matches the primary evidence. The
empirical hierarchy is therefore consistent from start to finish:
precision and magnitude from CFPS, sign corroboration from aggregate OLS,
and directional alignment from IV under weak-identification safeguards.
