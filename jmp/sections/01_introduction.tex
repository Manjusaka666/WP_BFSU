\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Household inflation expectations shape monetary policy
transmission, yet the cognitive process behind those
expectations remains contested. This paper tests whether
Chinese households overreact to salient inflation signals
in the manner predicted by diagnostic expectations
\citep{Bordalo2020b}, and whether the resulting forecast
errors are economically meaningful and predictable.

I find that they do. Using quarterly data from the People's
Bank of China Urban Depositor Survey (2011Q1--2025Q3) and
seven waves of the China Family Panel Studies
(CFPS, 2010--2022), I document a negative
revision--error coefficient at both the aggregate and
household levels. At the aggregate level, quarters in which
households sharply revise inflation expectations upward are
followed by negative forecast errors as realized inflation
undershoots the revised belief
($\hat{\beta}_1 = -2.24$, Newey--West $p = 0.055$, $N = 32$).
At the household level, the CFPS panel confirms this pattern
with substantially greater statistical power: the revision
coefficient is approximately $-0.55$
($p < 0.001$, $N \approx 13{,}700$) with province and wave
fixed effects. Both estimates carry the sign predicted by
diagnostic overreaction and are inconsistent with the
rational-expectations null of zero.

The empirical design addresses three challenges that
prior work on Chinese expectations has left open. First,
the Carlson--Parkin quantification of the PBoC's qualitative
survey introduces measurement dependence on the perception
threshold and the treatment of uncertain respondents. I
construct bound estimates that allocate uncertain respondents
to pessimistic and optimistic extremes, and I verify that
the diagnostic sign holds across all bounds and across
alternative threshold values. Second, the short aggregate
sample ($N = 32$ usable quarters) limits the power of
time-series tests and precludes sharp discrimination between
competing models in any single specification. I address this
by combining the aggregate evidence with the CFPS household
panel, where cross-sectional variation within
province-wave cells provides an independent test of the same
hypothesis at a different level of aggregation. Third,
separating diagnostic overreaction from information rigidity
\citep{Coibion2015} requires a specification that nests
both channels. I estimate a mechanism-competition regression
that includes both the forecast revision (diagnostic channel)
and the lagged forecast error (rigidity channel). In the
joint specification, the lagged-error term carries a
significant negative coefficient
($\hat{\lambda} = -0.60$, $p = 0.005$), while the revision
coefficient attenuates. The negative sign on lagged
errors---forecast errors reversing across consecutive
periods---is itself consistent with diagnostic dynamics:
overreaction in one period generates a correction in the
next. I interpret this as evidence that both channels
carry explanatory power, with their relative importance
varying across economic states.

A salience-IV design provides supplementary directional
evidence. I instrument inflation salience with media
congestion from geopolitical news, exploiting the logic
that geopolitical events crowd media bandwidth away from
inflation coverage. The reduced-form sign is consistent
with the diagnostic prediction ($-0.41$), though the first
stage is weak ($F = 1.16$), making the IV evidence
suggestive rather than definitive. The OLS diagnostic test
and the CFPS household-level analysis provide the primary
identification; the IV adds a direction-of-causality
check that reinforces the OLS sign without pinning
down magnitudes.

Dynamic evidence from local projections
\citep{Jorda2005} sharpens the timing: revision shocks
move forecast errors within one quarter and then attenuate,
while the realized-inflation response is smaller and fades
faster. This temporal asymmetry---fast overreaction followed
by partial correction---matches the diagnostic profile and
is difficult to generate under pure information rigidity,
where delayed correction should accumulate. A Bayesian
state-space model tracks the time-varying diagnostic
coefficient $\beta_t$, which intensifies during geopolitical
crises and pandemic onset and relaxes toward zero in calmer
periods. The time variation is economically interpretable
and survives prior sensitivity checks.

The estimates translate into a measurable policy tool.
A forecast-adjustment rule that corrects raw survey
expectations by the estimated overreaction component
reduces mean absolute error by 17\% and improves 68\%
interval coverage from 42\% to 67\% in rolling
out-of-sample backtests. The rule requires no structural
model of the inflation process and no parameters beyond
those already estimated, providing a real-time diagnostic
for when survey expectations are most likely to overstate
near-term inflation.

This paper contributes along two margins. First, I provide
the first household-level test of the diagnostic-expectations
hypothesis outside the United States and Europe, using a
setting where information frictions and food-price salience
are quantitatively larger than in developed-market surveys.
The CFPS microdata confirm that overreaction is pervasive
across demographic groups rather than concentrated among
particular subpopulations, with household-level revision
coefficients that are precisely estimated and robust to
province and wave fixed effects. Second, I introduce a
competing-mechanism design that nests diagnostic and
information-rigidity channels in the same specification,
applied to both aggregate and household-level data. This
design reveals that neither channel dominates unconditionally:
the diagnostic margin activates more strongly during
high-uncertainty episodes, while error-reversal persistence
carries explanatory power throughout the sample. The
evidence points toward a mixed expectation-formation process
in which cognitive overreaction and informational frictions
coexist, with their relative weight shifting across
macroeconomic states.

Three boundaries define where the results generalize and
where they do not. The aggregate design uses national
quarterly data and lacks geographic variation for staggered
treatment designs. The CFPS household panel provides
cross-sectional heterogeneity but observes price
expectations only biennially, limiting dynamic analysis at
the micro level. Media congestion is a plausible but not
experimentally assigned instrument, and the weak first
stage prevents sharp IV-based causal claims. These
constraints are explicit throughout the analysis and define
where future work with province-household matched data and
richer media microstructure can extend the identification
logic.
