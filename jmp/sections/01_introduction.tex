\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A one-point upward revision in household inflation expectations predicts a 0.55-point
more negative forecast error at the next interview, a slope that is economically large
for any model that maps survey beliefs into near-term household demand, credit choices,
and durable-goods timing. I ask a direct question: when salient inflation signals hit China, do households update close to a
rational benchmark, or do they overshoot and then correct? I show clear overreaction.
Using seven CFPS waves from 2010 to 2022, I estimate $\beta=-0.55$ (Table~\ref{tab:oster_bounds}) with $p<0.001$
and about $N\approx13{,}700$ household observations after province and wave fixed
effects. This elasticity does not come from one narrow subgroup; it appears across
income, education, age, and urban-rural cells, so the wedge between expected and
realized inflation is broad rather than idiosyncratic. The magnitude also implies a
practical forecasting consequence: many expectation revisions contain predictable mean
reversion at the relevant horizon, so raw revisions can overstate true inflation pressure
in real time, especially after salient food and energy news shocks. I therefore treat
overreaction as a first-order measurement problem for macroeconomists, not a side
detail, because the coefficient size is large enough to change inference about household
sentiment, policy credibility, policy reaction calibration, and short-run welfare losses
from misallocated intertemporal choices when transitory price spikes are mistaken for persistent inflation.

I make three contributions that move beyond a baseline correlation. First, I provide a
household diagnostic test in a large emerging economy where salience shocks and
information frictions are both quantitatively important. This is micro evidence at the
unit that forms expectations: I observe revisions and subsequent errors for the same
households, and I recover a stable negative revision-error elasticity in within-cell
variation after rich fixed effects, alternative survey weights, and multiple coding
choices for qualitative responses. The contribution is external validity with discipline:
the mechanism survives in a setting with different inflation composition, media ecology,
and policy communication institutions from the environments where this framework was
first studied. Second, I run a mechanism competition that places diagnostic overreaction
and information-rigidity dynamics in one empirical system and asks which channel
explains forecast errors conditional on the other. I find coexistence instead of a single
winner; persistence terms matter, but revision-driven overshooting remains central when
salience is high, which means channel weight is state dependent and policy-relevant
nonlinearity is empirically present. This matters for policy design because communication
should damp short-run spikes exactly when salience intensifies, not only re-anchor long-run beliefs. Third, I run a CHFS null channel test
that asks whether persistent differences in media access alone generate stronger belief
levels in cross section. The estimated channel slope is $\beta=-0.005$ (Table~\ref{tab:chfs_mechanism_info_channel}, column~1) with $SE=0.022$,
which is economically tiny and statistically close to zero even under flexible controls.
I interpret this null as a useful boundary condition: the large CFPS overreaction
pattern is more consistent with event-triggered salience shocks than with a time-invariant ranking of households by habitual information channel or fixed attention
gaps tied to media technology alone.

I also state the limits clearly so claims match evidence. The aggregate corroboration
sample is short, with $N=32$ quarterly observations, so aggregate regressions can
confirm sign and timing but cannot pin down precise macro elasticities under many
controls or discriminate tightly across nearby models. The CFPS expectation variable is biennial, which restricts high-frequency dynamic tracing and compresses adjustment into longer windows than the mechanism likely
operates. My salience IV has conceptual appeal but weak power in this sample: the
first-stage statistic is $F=1.16$ (Table~\ref{tab:identification_main}, Panel~A), so IV magnitudes are not a credible basis for sharp
causal parameter claims and I treat them as directional checks only. The CHFS test is
a single cross-section, so I cannot follow within-household revisions over time there or
separate persistent unobserved heterogeneity from channel exposure with panel leverage
or test dynamic channel switching after shocks.
These constraints narrow interpretation but do not overturn the core fact: the CFPS
panel repeatedly shows a large, negative revision-error elasticity with robust precision.
The limits instead define what I can and cannot claim. I can claim economically
meaningful household overreaction in reduced form, state-dependent mechanism coexistence
from direct horse races, and a rejected channel-only interpretation in CHFS. I cannot
claim point-identified deep structural primitives from the weak-IV aggregate design or
from one CHFS cross section. That disciplined boundary keeps the contribution strong on
magnitudes, explicit about uncertainty, transparent on identification, and useful for designing richer next-wave data collection.
