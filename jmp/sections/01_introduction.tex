\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Households in China overreact to inflation signals. Using seven waves of the China
Family Panel Studies (CFPS, 2010--2022), I regress each household's forecast error on
its prior expectation revision and estimate $\beta = -0.55$ ($p < 0.001$,
$N \approx 13{,}000$) with province $\times$ wave fixed effects
(Table~\ref{tab:cfps_baseline}, column X). The sign is unambiguous: a one-point
upward revision in expected inflation predicts a 0.55-point more negative forecast
error at the next interview.

This sign pattern is consistent with reversal rather than monotone persistence:
households that revise expected inflation upward subsequently overpredict inflation.
The pattern does not come from one narrow subgroup. It appears across income
quartiles, education levels, age bins, and the urban-rural divide, so the wedge
between expected and realized inflation is broad rather than idiosyncratic.

China is a useful setting for studying household expectation formation. Food prices,
especially pork, are salient to consumers and volatile enough to generate large
belief revisions; the 2018--2019 African Swine Fever episode is one recent example.
At the same time, monetary policy communication from the People's Bank of China
remains less transparent than in major inflation-targeting central banks, so
households receive strong price signals but weak anchoring guidance. This combination
makes both overreaction and slow updating plausible ex ante, which means the data can
discriminate between them rather than merely confirm a prior.

I make three contributions. First, I establish the main fact at the household level:
individuals who revise inflation expectations upward subsequently make more negative
forecast errors, consistent with overshooting. This holds across demographic and
geographic cells in the CFPS panel with stable magnitude after alternative survey
weights and multiple coding choices for qualitative responses. Second, I provide
aggregate corroboration using quarterly PBoC depositor-survey data, which yield
$\beta = -2.24$ ($p = 0.055$, $N = 32$) (Table~\ref{tab:ols_baseline}, column 2).
The sign matches the household-level result, and the timing aligns with quarters of
salient food-price news. Third, I estimate a joint specification that nests both
overreaction and persistence channels to ask which explains forecast errors
conditional on the other. Error reversals remain present after controlling for
persistence, more consistent with overreaction than with pure sluggish updating. A
supplementary test using the China Household Finance Survey (CHFS) asks whether
persistent differences in media exposure alone explain belief intensity in cross
section. The estimated media-channel slope is $\beta = -0.005$ ($SE = 0.022$)
(Table~\ref{tab:chfs_mechanism_info_channel}, column 1), economically negligible and
statistically indistinguishable from zero, which suggests that overreaction is
event-driven rather than a fixed function of information access.

I state the limits of this paper explicitly. I do not claim point identification of
a structural diagnostic-expectations parameter. I do not claim a decisive test
against all information-rigidity models. I do not claim a policy-invariant welfare
mapping from overreaction to output losses. The aggregate sample is short ($N = 32$),
so macro regressions confirm sign and timing but cannot pin down precise elasticities.
The CFPS expectation variable is biennial and qualitative, which compresses
adjustment dynamics and limits high-frequency tracing. The salience IV has weak
first-stage power ($F = 1.16$, Table~\ref{tab:identification_main}, Panel A), so I
treat IV estimates as directional checks, not as a basis for sharp causal claims. The
CHFS test is a single cross-section without panel leverage. These constraints narrow
interpretation but do not overturn the core household-level fact: a large, negative,
and precisely estimated revision-error elasticity that replicates across subsamples.
