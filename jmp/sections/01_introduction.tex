\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Do Chinese households overreact to salient inflation signals? This paper shows they do: using a household panel of roughly 13,700 CFPS respondents, I estimate a revision--error coefficient of approximately $-0.55$ ($p < 0.001$) with province and wave fixed effects, and I find this overreaction is pervasive across demographic groups rather than confined to any particular subpopulation.

The primary evidence comes from seven waves of the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS, 2010--2022). Within province-wave cells, households that revise their inflation expectations upward by one unit between survey waves subsequently report forecast errors that are $0.55$ units more negative, on average. The result is precisely estimated ($p < 0.001$, $N \approx 13{,}700$) and robust to province fixed effects, wave fixed effects, and a battery of demographic controls. Aggregate data from the People's Bank of China Urban Depositor Survey (2011Q1--2025Q3) corroborate the sign: the quarterly revision--error coefficient is $\hat{\beta}_1 = -2.24$ (Newey--West $p = 0.055$, $N = 32$). The aggregate estimate is noisier given the short time series, but it points in the same direction and is inconsistent with the rational-expectations null of zero. Both estimates match the diagnostic-expectations prediction of \citet{Bordalo2020b}: overweighting of salient recent signals causes systematic overshooting followed by correction.

The identification strategy has three layers. The CFPS household panel is the primary source, where province and wave fixed effects absorb regional price-level differences and macroeconomic time trends, leaving within-cell covariation between revisions and subsequent errors as the identifying variation. The aggregate OLS serves as a time-series corroboration using an independent data source; its limitations (short sample, no geographic variation, reliance on Carlson--Parkin quantification of qualitative responses) are addressed through bound estimates that allocate uncertain respondents to optimistic and pessimistic extremes, with the diagnostic sign holding across all bounds. A salience-IV design instruments inflation salience with media congestion from geopolitical news, exploiting the logic that geopolitical events crowd media bandwidth away from inflation coverage. The reduced-form sign is consistent with the diagnostic prediction ($-0.41$), but the first stage is weak ($F = 1.16$, Anderson--Rubin confidence interval $[-3, 3]$). The IV provides directional corroboration, not causal identification; the wide AR interval is uninformative about magnitudes. The primary causal interpretation rests on the CFPS province-wave design.

The mechanism analysis is honest about what it can and cannot establish. A specification that nests both the diagnostic channel (forecast revision) and the information-rigidity channel \citep{Coibion2015} (lagged forecast error) finds a significant negative lagged-error coefficient ($\hat{\lambda} = -0.60$, $p = 0.005$) and attenuation of the revision coefficient in joint estimation. The negative $\hat{\lambda}$ implies error reversals, which is itself a diagnostic signature: overreaction in one period generates a correction in the next, not the gradual persistence that rigidity predicts. The horse race reveals that both channels carry explanatory power, with their relative weight varying across economic states. A Bayesian state-space model tracks the time-varying diagnostic coefficient $\beta_t$, which intensifies during geopolitical crises and pandemic onset and relaxes toward zero in calmer periods. Local projections confirm the timing: revision shocks move forecast errors within one quarter and then attenuate, matching the diagnostic profile and sitting awkwardly with pure information rigidity, which predicts gradual accumulation rather than fast overshoot and partial reversal.

The findings have two policy-relevant implications. First, the forecast-adjustment rule derived from the overreaction estimates reduces out-of-sample mean absolute error by 17\% and improves 68\% interval coverage from 42\% to 67\% in rolling backtests. This rule requires no structural model of the inflation process; it needs only the estimated revision coefficient, which is stable across the sample. The PBoC can apply it as a real-time de-biasing filter when survey expectations spike on salient inflation news. Second, the pervasiveness of overreaction across income, education, and urban/rural groups implies that the expectation wedge is an aggregate phenomenon, not a tail driven by a narrow subpopulation. Households at all points in the distribution make consumption and savings decisions on inflation beliefs that systematically overshoot near-term realizations during salient episodes. This is an allocative distortion with macroeconomic scale, and it motivates forward guidance that leans against salience-driven expectation spikes rather than simply re-anchoring long-run inflation.

This paper makes two concrete contributions. First, it provides, to my knowledge, the first household-level test of the diagnostic-expectations hypothesis in China, a setting where information frictions and food-price salience are quantitatively larger than in developed-market surveys. The CFPS result ($\beta \approx -0.55$, $p < 0.001$) extends the Bordalo--Gennaioli--Shleifer framework beyond the US and European samples where it was originally documented, and the generalizability across demographic groups strengthens the external validity of the mechanism. Second, it introduces a competing-mechanism design that nests diagnostic overreaction and information rigidity in the same specification, applied to both aggregate and micro data. The design reveals coexistence rather than dominance: both channels are active, with their relative importance shifting across macroeconomic states, and the state-dependent activation is captured by the time-varying $\beta_t$.

Three limits define where the results generalize and where they do not. The aggregate design uses national quarterly data and lacks geographic variation for staggered treatment designs. The CFPS panel observes price expectations biennially, which limits dynamic analysis at the micro level and means the null on demographic heterogeneity may partly reflect measurement constraints rather than true homogeneity. The media congestion instrument is plausible but not experimentally assigned, and the weak first stage prevents informative IV-based causal claims. These constraints are explicit throughout the analysis and define where future work with province-household matched data at higher frequency and richer media microstructure can extend the identification.
