\section{Robustness to Ordinal Measurement}\label{app:scaling}

\subsection{The Ordinal Measurement Problem}

The CFPS inflation expectation question elicits direction, not
magnitude.
Respondents report whether they expect prices to rise, stay the
same, or fall, coded as $\mu_{it} \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$.
This is a categorical indicator, not a point forecast.
A respondent who reports ``prices will rise'' could mean
1 percent or 20 percent; the survey provides no way to
distinguish these.

The forecast error
$\mathrm{FE}_{ipt} = \pi_{p,\,t\to t+1} - \mu_{ipt}$
therefore subtracts an ordinal code from a continuous variable
measured in annual percentage points.
The resulting object is well-defined as a composite variable,
but its units are not interpretable as a standard forecast
error because the two components are on different scales.

This measurement limitation is shared by all papers that use
qualitative survey expectations: the Michigan Survey
``up/down/same'' question, the EU Consumer Survey balance
statistic, and the PBoC Urban Depositor Survey.
The limitation affects the \textit{magnitude} of the
forecast-error coefficient but not the conclusions of the
paper, for two reasons developed below: the sign of
$\hat{\beta}_3$ is invariant to any monotone rescaling of the
ordinal variable, and the pass-through coefficient
$\hat{\beta}_2$ provides a scaling-free magnitude test.

\subsection{Sign Invariance Under Monotone Rescaling}

Let $f\colon \{-1, 0, 1\} \to \mathbb{R}$ be any strictly
increasing function.
Define the rescaled forecast error
$\mathrm{FE}^f_{ipt} = \pi_{p,\,t\to t+1} - f(\mu_{it})$.
In a regression of $\mathrm{FE}^f$ on the meat shock $s_{pt}$
with controls $X$, the coefficient decomposes as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sign_decomp}
  \beta_3(f)
  = \underbrace{\beta_2}_{\text{pass-through}}
  - \underbrace{%
      \frac{\Cov(f(\mu_{it}),\, s_{pt} \mid X)}%
           {\Var(s_{pt} \mid X)}%
    }_{\text{scaled expectation response}}.
\end{equation}
Two empirical facts pin down the sign.
Equation~2 establishes that $\beta_2 < 0$: provinces
with larger meat shocks experienced \textit{lower} subsequent
headline inflation (Table~\ref{tab:eq2_cpi_passthrough}).
Equation~1 establishes that $\mu_{it}$ is positively
correlated with $s_{pt}$: households in high-shock provinces
report ``rise'' more often
(Table~\ref{tab:eq1_expectations}).
For any strictly increasing $f$, this positive correlation is
preserved, so $\Cov(f(\mu),\, s \mid X) > 0$.

Combining these two facts:
\[
  \beta_3(f) = \underbrace{\beta_2}_{<\,0}
  - \underbrace{\text{(positive term)}}_{>\,0} < 0.
\]
The sign of $\hat{\beta}_3$ is negative regardless of the
specific values assigned to each ordinal category, as long as
the mapping preserves the ordering
(``fall'' $<$ ``same'' $<$ ``rise'').
This result is not a tautology; it follows from the joint
empirical structure of Equations~1 and 2.
But once those two signs are established, the overreaction
conclusion is immune to the scaling choice.

\subsection{A Scaling-Free Magnitude Test}

The magnitude of $\hat{\beta}_3$ depends on the scaling, but
the paper's conclusions do not require interpreting
$|\hat{\beta}_3|$ at face value.
The pass-through coefficient $\hat{\beta}_2 = -5.25$
($p = 0.007$; Table~\ref{tab:eq2_cpi_passthrough}, column~4)
is measured entirely in CPI percentage points and involves no
ordinal variable.

Under rational expectations, the forecast-error coefficient
satisfies $|\beta_3^R| \leq \omega H \approx 1.20$
(Section~\ref{sec:time_aggregation}).
The decomposition in equation~\eqref{eq:sign_decomp} shows
that setting $f(\mu) = 0$ for all respondents (i.e., ignoring
expectations entirely) yields $\beta_3(f) = \beta_2$.
Even under this extreme assumption, $|\hat{\beta}_2| = 5.25$
exceeds the time-aggregated rational bound of 1.20 by a factor
of four.

This means the magnitude comparison does not depend on
the scaling.
The CPI pass-through channel alone is large enough to reject
the rational benchmark.
The ordinal expectation variable adds further overreaction
on top, but the scaling-free lower bound is already decisive.

\subsection{Sensitivity Across Scaling Functions}

Table~\ref{tab:scaling_sensitivity} reports $\hat{\beta}_3$
under three alternative scalings of the ordinal variable to
confirm that the coefficient remains negative, large, and
statistically significant regardless of the mapping chosen.
The purpose is not to identify the ``correct'' scaling (no
scaling is correct, because the survey does not elicit a point
forecast) but to verify that the result is not an artifact of
a particular coding convention.

In all cases, the coefficient exceeds the time-aggregated
rational bound of 1.20 by a factor of at least four.

\subsection{Carlson-Parkin Quantification}\label{app:carlson_parkin}

As a further check, I apply the \citet{Carlson1975}
method to convert the ordinal responses into quantitative
expected inflation rates.
The method assumes that the underlying distribution of
individual expected inflation within each province-wave cell is
normal, and uses the observed shares of ``rise,'' ``same,'' and
``fall'' responses together with realised inflation to back out
the implied mean expected inflation $\hat{\mu}^{CP}_{pt}$ for
each cell.

Specifically, let $\Phi^{-1}(\cdot)$ denote the standard normal
quantile function, and let $p^{-}_{pt}$, $p^{0}_{pt}$, and
$p^{+}_{pt}$ denote the shares of respondents in province $p$
at wave $t$ who report ``fall,'' ``same,'' and ``rise.''
The indifference thresholds are
$a_{pt} = \Phi^{-1}(p^{-}_{pt})$ and
$b_{pt} = \Phi^{-1}(1 - p^{+}_{pt})$.
Using realised CPI inflation $\pi_{pt}$ as the scaling
anchor, the implied expected inflation is
\[
  \hat{\mu}^{CP}_{pt}
  = \pi_{pt} \cdot \frac{a_{pt} + b_{pt}}{b_{pt} - a_{pt}},
\]
and the Carlson-Parkin forecast error is
$\mathrm{FE}^{CP}_{pt} = \pi_{pt} - \hat{\mu}^{CP}_{pt}$.

This construction puts both components in the same units
(annual percentage points), producing a forecast error
that is directly comparable to the rational benchmark.

\input{\tabdir/eq3_carlson_parkin}

Table~\ref{tab:eq3_carlson_parkin} reports the results.
The Carlson-Parkin forecast-error coefficient under the
preferred specification is $\hat{\beta}_3^{CP} = -7.61$
($p < 0.001$), close to the baseline estimate.
The coefficient is negative and statistically significant at
the one-percent level in all four full-sample specifications.
The $R^2$ values are higher than in the baseline regressions
because the Carlson-Parkin forecast error is measured at the
province-wave level, removing within-cell variation.
The conclusion is unchanged: the overreaction finding does not
depend on an arbitrary ordinal-to-cardinal mapping.

\subsection{Ordered Probit}\label{app:ordered_probit}

A complementary approach avoids the forecast-error construction
altogether.
An ordered probit regression models the probability of
reporting ``rise'' versus ``same'' versus ``fall'' as a
function of the meat shock, without assigning cardinal values
to the categories.
The ordered probit coefficient on the meat shock is $0.354$
($p < 0.001$) under region-by-wave fixed effects, confirming
that meat-price shocks shift the distribution of ordinal
responses toward ``rise.''

Combined with the negative pass-through ($\hat{\beta}_2 < 0$),
this establishes the overreaction pattern without any scaling
assumption: households move their beliefs in the direction
opposite to what the shock actually predicts for future
headline inflation.

\subsection{Summary}

The overreaction finding rests on three results that are
individually robust to the ordinal measurement limitation:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The \textit{sign} of $\hat{\beta}_3 < 0$ is invariant
    to any monotone rescaling of the ordinal expectation
    (Section~A5.2).
  \item The \textit{magnitude} of $|\hat{\beta}_2| = 5.25$
    exceeds the rational bound without involving the ordinal
    variable at all (Section~A5.3).
  \item The \textit{Carlson-Parkin} quantification, which
    converts ordinal responses to cardinal units using the
    cross-sectional response distribution, yields
    $\hat{\beta}_3^{CP} = -7.61$ ($p < 0.001$) under the
    preferred specification (Section~A5.5).
\end{enumerate}
The ordinal scale introduces imprecision in the exact magnitude
of $\hat{\beta}_3$, but the qualitative conclusion is
unambiguous under every approach considered.
