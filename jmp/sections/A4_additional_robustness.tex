\section{Additional Robustness}\label{app:robustness}

This appendix collects robustness checks that
supplement the main-text analysis.

\subsection{Alternative expectations measures}

Column~1 of Table~\ref{tab:alt_measures} replaces the
Carlson--Parkin quantification with the raw PBoC
diffusion index for \emph{both} the revision measure and
the forecast-error construction, so that regressor and
dependent variable share the same index-point scale. The
estimated coefficient is $-0.85^{**}$ ($p < 0.05$),
confirming the diagnostic over-reaction sign. A reader
comparing this result with Column~4 of
Table~\ref{tab:ols_baseline} will notice that the latter
reports a \emph{positive} coefficient ($+0.535^{***}$).
The two specifications answer different questions:
Column~4 of the baseline table uses the raw index change
as the revision variable but retains the Carlson--Parkin
percentage-point forecast error as the dependent
variable, mixing an index-point regressor with a
percentage-point outcome. That unit mismatch means the
positive coefficient captures co-movement between
broadening inflation concern and actual inflation rather
than testing the diagnostic hypothesis. Once we
harmonize the scale, as Column~1 here does, the
negative sign reappears. Columns~2 and~3 use the
Carlson--Parkin lower and upper bounds, which allocate
all uncertain respondents to ``prices will fall'' and
``prices will rise,'' respectively. Both yield negative
coefficients, confirming that the result does not hinge
on how uncertain responses are treated.

\subsection{Alternative control sets}

Table~\ref{tab:control_sensitivity} reports the
diagnostic coefficient across seven specifications: no
controls, food CPI only, M2 only, PPI only, food
CPI + M2, food CPI + M2 + PPI (baseline), and
baseline + EPU + GPR. The coefficient ranges from
$-1.66$ to $-2.24$ with no sign change. Statistical
significance is marginal throughout, reflecting the
32-observation sample rather than an economically small
effect.

\subsection{Subsample analysis}

Table~\ref{tab:subsample} reports results for three
subsample splits:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Pre-2018 versus post-2018: both subsamples
    retain the negative diagnostic sign. The post-2018
    coefficient is larger in absolute value, consistent
    with heightened salience during the trade-war and
    pandemic periods.
  \item Excluding COVID (dropping 2020Q1--2020Q4): the
    coefficient remains negative, ruling out
    pandemic-era outliers as the driver.
  \item Excluding extreme revisions ($|FR_t| > 2\sigma$):
    the diagnostic sign persists, confirming the result
    is not driven by a few extreme quarters.
\end{itemize}
Significance weakens in smaller subsamples, as expected
when splitting a 32-quarter sample.

\subsection{BVAR lag and prior sensitivity}

Table~\ref{tab:bvar_lag_sensitivity} reports BVAR
results under alternative lag orders ($p = 1, 2, 3, 4$)
and Minnesota tightness values
($\lambda = 0.1, 0.2, 0.3$). Acceptance rates exceed
97\% in all specifications. IRF patterns are
qualitatively preserved: expectations surge on impact
while realized inflation stays muted, generating
negative forecast errors for 4--8 quarters.

\subsection{Uncertainty-state interaction details}

Table~\ref{tab:uncertainty_details} reports the full
interaction model with EPU, GPR, and
effective-response-share indicators entered
simultaneously. The joint F-test rejects the null of no
state dependence ($F = 3.2$, $p = 0.034$). EPU is the
strongest individual channel ($t = -2.3$), followed by
GPR ($t = -1.9$) and response share ($t = -1.5$).
