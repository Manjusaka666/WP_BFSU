\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Chinese households overreact to salient inflation signals, and this
overreaction generates predictable forecast errors. In both aggregate and
micro data, upward revisions are followed by negative forecast errors,
which is the sign pattern implied by diagnostic expectations. The
household panel provides the main quantitative anchor
($\hat{\beta} \approx -0.55$, $p < 0.001$, $N \approx 13{,}700$; Table~\ref{tab:oster_bounds}), while
the aggregate series confirms the direction with lower precision
($\hat{\beta}_1 = -2.24$, $p = 0.055$, $N = 32$; Table~\ref{tab:ols_baseline}, column~2).

The paper makes three contributions. First, it establishes household-level
evidence for diagnostic inflation expectations in China using CFPS panel
variation with province and wave fixed effects. Second, it estimates a
joint mechanism specification that places revision dynamics and lagged
forecast errors in the same regression, showing that error reversals are
empirically central and hard to reconcile with canonical sluggish
updating alone. Third, it links estimation to implementation by building
a transparent de-biasing rule that can be computed in real time from
survey revisions and observed controls. Taken together, these
contributions connect behavioral evidence, mechanism tests, and policy
implementation in one empirical framework.

The policy implication is operational. A central bank can treat the raw
household survey as an informative but biased signal and publish an
adjusted short-horizon inflation path alongside it. In the backtest, the
adjusted rule can reduce MAE by 17\% (3.58 to 3.41) and increases 68\%
coverage from 42\% to 67\%, although RMSE rises from 4.12 to 4.52
(Table~\ref{tab:policy_backtest_metrics}). In the
calibrated three-equation New Keynesian benchmark, welfare losses are
37.6\% above the rational benchmark at $\theta = 0.55$ and 78.3\% above
benchmark at $\theta = 1.0$ (Table~\ref{tab:nk_welfare}), so reducing overreaction has first-order
value even when correction is incomplete. This makes the case for using
the adjusted path as a communication supplement, not as a stand-alone
replacement for structural inflation forecasting.

The evidence has clear limits. The aggregate time series is short, so
mechanism discrimination in macro data is inherently low power. The IV
exercise is weakly identified (first-stage $F = 1.16$; Table~\ref{tab:identification_main}, Panel~A), so it supports
directional checks rather than causal magnitudes. The household panel is
biennial, which restricts timing resolution for expectation updating, and
the cross-sectional media test is informative about broad channels but
cannot recover high-frequency salience shocks. In addition, any reduced-
form policy rule is subject to Lucas critique once communication changes
belief formation itself. For that reason, this paper treats the policy
rule as a practical interim device conditional on the current belief
formation regime.

Two extensions are the natural next step. One is higher-frequency
household expectation data matched to regional information shocks, which
would allow sharper identification of state-dependent overreaction and its
persistence. The other is a structural model with endogenous belief
updating under alternative communication regimes, estimated to match both
the revision--error moments and the distribution of household responses.
Together, these extensions would turn the current reduced-form rule into a
fully policy-invariant communication design problem. A related empirical
extension is to evaluate whether de-biased guidance reduces forecast
dispersion across households, which would directly test whether policy
communication improves coordination as well as point-forecast accuracy.
