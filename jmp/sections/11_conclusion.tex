\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Households exposed to large pork-price shocks systematically
overpredict future inflation.
The forecast errors are negative, large, and robust: provinces
with bigger meat-price increases between CFPS waves had households
whose inflation expectations exceeded realised headline CPI by a
wider margin at the next wave.
The result survives wild cluster bootstrap inference with
31 province clusters and is stable across progressive additions
of province, wave, and region-by-wave fixed effects.

The overreaction has a clean decomposition.
Meat-price shocks raise household expectations but do not raise
forward headline CPI at the province level.
The gap between the expectation response and the price-level
response is the forecast error.
This two-equation structure separates the belief channel from the
price-level channel and avoids the mechanical negative correlation
that contaminates standard revision-error designs, where the level
of the expectation enters both the revision and the error by
construction.

Three features of the evidence discipline the mechanism.
The commodity placebo test shows that grain shocks of
comparable statistical magnitude do not produce the same
expectation response, ruling out generic food-price
confounding and pointing to a salience channel specific to
frequently purchased, cognitively prominent goods.
The estimated expectation response exceeds the rational
benchmark by a large margin: meat's official CPI weight implies
a rational response of roughly $0.05$, while the point estimate
is several times larger.
The demographic interactions indicate that the
overreaction is broad-based across education, income, and
urban-rural groups rather than concentrated in any single
demographic stratum.

These findings bear on how central banks interpret survey-based
inflation expectations.
If households systematically extrapolate from salient
relative-price movements, raw survey measures overstate the
inflation signal embedded in actual price dynamics.
The PBoC, which publishes quarterly depositor-survey expectations
as one input to its policy deliberations, faces this problem
directly.
Adjusting for the salience wedge documented here could improve
the signal content of survey expectations as a policy input.

Several limitations apply.
The CFPS is biennial, so the analysis cannot trace within-year
adjustment dynamics or test whether overreaction concentrates
in the months immediately following a shock.
Province-level meat CPI conflates supply and demand movements;
ASF provides a plausible supply instrument, but the treatment
is an imperfect proxy for the supply component alone.
The ordinal three-point expectation measure in the CFPS limits
the precision with which the expectation response can be
quantified in CPI units.
With 31 province clusters, all inference relies on the wild
cluster bootstrap.

Two extensions follow naturally.
Higher-frequency household expectation data matched to
commodity-price signals at a finer geographic level would allow
sharper identification of the adjustment dynamics and the
persistence of the salience effect.
A structural model of belief formation under diagnostic
expectations, calibrated to the reduced-form estimates presented
here, could quantify the welfare cost of the expectation wedge
and evaluate whether targeted communication about relative-price
versus aggregate-price movements reduces the overreaction.
