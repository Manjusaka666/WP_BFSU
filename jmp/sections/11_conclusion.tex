\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper shows that Chinese households overreact to inflation signals.
Upward revisions in expectations are followed by negative forecast errors,
a pattern that holds in both household panel data and aggregate PBoC
survey data. The CFPS panel estimate is $\hat{\beta} \approx -0.55$
($p < 0.001$, $N \approx 13{,}000$) (Table~\ref{tab:cfps_baseline}, column X),
while the aggregate series confirms the sign with lower precision
($N = 32$) (Table~\ref{tab:ols_baseline}, column 2). The direction is more
consistent with reversal-based overreaction than with pure sluggish updating.

I make three contributions relative to the existing literature. First, I
provide the first household-level evidence for diagnostic inflation
expectations in China, exploiting CFPS panel variation with province $\times$
wave fixed effects. Second, I estimate a joint specification that places
revision dynamics and lagged forecast errors in the same regression. Error
reversals are empirically central, which is difficult to reconcile with
canonical noisy information models alone. Third, I use a cross-sectional
test from the CHFS to examine whether persistent media exposure drives the
pattern. The null result on that channel is consistent with event-driven
overreaction rather than a sustained information supply effect.

The evidence has clear limits. The aggregate time series contains only 32
observations (Table~\ref{tab:ols_baseline}, column 2), so mechanism
discrimination at the macro level is inherently low power. The CFPS is
biennial, which restricts timing resolution for expectation updating. The
instrumental variable exercise is weakly identified (first-stage
$F = 1.16$) (Table~\ref{tab:identification_main}, Panel A), so it supports
directional consistency rather than precise causal magnitudes. The CHFS
mechanism test relies on a single cross-section, limiting what can be said
about dynamic media effects. Finally, any reduced-form forecasting rule
built on these estimates is subject to the Lucas critique: if the PBoC
changes its communication strategy in response to the overreaction finding,
the belief formation process itself may shift.

These findings have implications for how survey-based inflation forecasts
are interpreted. Raw household surveys are informative but biased signals.
Appendix~\ref{app:forecasting} shows that a simple correction rule built
from the estimated revision-error relationship can improve directional
accuracy in an expanding-window backtest (Table~\ref{tab:policy_backtest_metrics};
Figure~\ref{fig:policy_backtest}), though the adjustment should be treated
as a practical interim device rather than a structural forecast.

Several directions for future work follow naturally. Higher-frequency
household expectation data matched to regional information shocks would
allow sharper identification of state-dependent overreaction and its
persistence. A structural model with endogenous belief updating under
alternative communication regimes could turn the reduced-form estimates
into policy-invariant design parameters. It would also be valuable to
evaluate whether de-biased guidance reduces forecast dispersion across
households, which would test directly whether improved communication
enhances coordination beyond point-forecast accuracy.
