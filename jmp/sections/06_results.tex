\section{Main Results}\label{sec:results}

The primary finding is that province-level meat-price shocks
generate large, negative household forecast errors that lie
well above the rational benchmark in absolute value.
I present the forecast-error result first
(Section~\ref{sec:eq3_results}), then decompose it into the
expectation channel (Section~\ref{sec:eq1_results}) and the
pass-through channel (Section~\ref{sec:eq2_results}).
Section~\ref{sec:calibration} compares the estimated
coefficient against the rational bound.
Section~\ref{sec:event_study} visualises the treatment
variation.

% -------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Meat Shocks Generate Systematic Forecast Errors}%
\label{sec:eq3_results}
% -------------------------------------------------------------

\input{\tabdir/eq3_forecast_error}

Table~\ref{tab:eq3_forecast_error} reports estimates of
equation~\eqref{eq:eq3}.
The dependent variable is realised province-level headline CPI
inflation minus the household's inflation expectation.
A negative value indicates overprediction: the household
expected more inflation than materialised.

The coefficient on the meat shock is negative in every column,
and statistically significant at the one-percent level in the
four specifications that use the full sample (columns 1, 3,
4, and 6).
The bivariate estimate (column~1) is $\hat{\beta}_3 = -1.97$.
Adding province-plus-wave fixed effects (column~3) raises
the absolute value to $5.35$; region-by-wave fixed effects
alone (column~4) give $5.56$.
The preferred specification adds province fixed effects on top
of region-by-wave fixed effects (column~6), yielding
$\hat{\beta}_3 = -7.13$ (bootstrap $p < 0.001$).

The monotone increase in $|\hat{\beta}_3|$ as more fixed
effects enter is the opposite of what one would expect if
the result were driven by omitted confounds.
Absorbing province-level permanent heterogeneity and
region-by-wave shocks strengthens, rather than attenuates,
the finding.

Columns~2 and 5 add household demographics.
Both are imprecise because the demographic subsample drops to
22,121 observations.
The point estimates retain the correct sign; the precision
loss is mechanical given a 68 percent reduction in sample
size with the same number of clusters.

The preferred estimate has a clear economic interpretation.
Consider two provinces in the same region observed in the same
wave; one experienced a cumulative meat CPI increase of
0.5 log points (about 65 percent), the other 0.3 log points
(about 35 percent).
The difference in treatment is 0.2 log points.
The predicted difference in forecast errors is
$-7.13 \times 0.2 = -1.43$: households in the higher-shock
province overpredict inflation by 1.43 percentage points more,
roughly three-quarters of the average headline CPI rate during
the sample period.

\subsection{Decomposition: Expectations}%
\label{sec:eq1_results}
% -------------------------------------------------------------

\input{\tabdir/eq1_expectations}

Table~\ref{tab:eq1_expectations} asks whether meat-price shocks
shift expectations (equation~\eqref{eq:eq1}).
The coefficient is positive in all six columns.

The bivariate estimate (column~1) is
$\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.028$ ($p = 0.02$): a one-unit increase in
the cumulative meat log-change shifts the ordinal expectation
upward by 0.028 units.
Under province-plus-wave fixed effects (column~3), the
coefficient rises to $0.090$ ($p = 0.088$).
Region-by-wave fixed effects alone (column~4) yield a larger
point estimate of $0.242$ but with wider confidence intervals
($p = 0.237$).

The preferred specification (column~6) adds province fixed
effects to region-by-wave fixed effects:
$\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.119$ ($p = 0.024$).
The province fixed effects absorb permanent cross-province
differences in the share of respondents reporting ``up,''
improving precision relative to the region-by-wave-only
specification.
The expectation channel is now statistically significant at
the five-percent level, confirming that meat-price shocks move
household beliefs, not only forecast errors.

The expectation result complements the forecast-error test.
By itself, $\hat{\beta}_3 < 0$ could arise either because
households raised expectations too much or because realised
CPI fell.
The positive $\hat{\beta}_1$ confirms that the numerator of the
forecast error (expectations) moved in the wrong direction,
not only the denominator (realised CPI).

\subsection{Decomposition: Pass-Through}%
\label{sec:eq2_results}
% -------------------------------------------------------------

\input{\tabdir/eq2_cpi_passthrough}

Table~\ref{tab:eq2_cpi_passthrough} estimates
equation~\eqref{eq:eq2} at the province$\times$wave level
(93 observations).
The point estimate is negative in all columns: provinces with
larger meat-price shocks experienced lower, not higher,
forward headline inflation.

The bivariate estimate is $\hat{\beta}_2 = -1.81$ ($p < 0.001$).
Province-plus-wave fixed effects (column~2) yield $-4.70$
($p = 0.008$); the preferred province-plus-region-by-wave
specification (column~4) yields $\hat{\beta}_2 = -5.25$
($p = 0.007$).

The negative $\beta_2$ reflects the ASF supply-shock reversal.
The ASF shock contracted pork supply in 2018--2019, raising
meat prices sharply.
As herds recovered through 2020--2022, the same provinces saw
the sharpest price reversals: meat CPI fell fastest where it
had risen most.
Because meat carries a non-trivial weight in headline CPI
(roughly 3--6 percent), the reversal dragged down headline CPI
in high-shock provinces.

This reversal is central to the overreaction interpretation.
Households in high-shock provinces observed large meat-price
increases and revised their inflation expectations upward.
But the price signal was temporary: future headline inflation in
their province was not higher but lower.
The household overreacted in the sense that it treated a
transitory shock as informative about future inflation when
the shock predicted the opposite.

\subsection{Comparison with the Rational Benchmark}%
\label{sec:calibration}
% -------------------------------------------------------------

\input{\tabdir/calibration_benchmark}

Table~\ref{tab:calibration_benchmark} compares the estimated
$\hat{\beta}_3$ against the rational benchmark derived in
Section~\ref{sec:rational_benchmark}.

Under the one-period rational benchmark, the forecast-error
coefficient satisfies
$|\beta_3^R| \leq \omega = 0.05$ for any persistence belief
$\hat{\rho} \in [0,1]$.
Section~\ref{sec:time_aggregation} extends this to the
time-aggregated empirical object: even allowing a single
concentrated monthly shock with a 24-month forward horizon,
the most generous rational bound is $24\omega = 1.20$.
The estimated $\hat{\beta}_3 = -7.13$ exceeds this
time-aggregated bound by a factor of six.

The table calibrates the benchmark under four persistence
scenarios.
Under any scenario, the rational worst-case bound lies between
zero and $-0.05$.
A fifth row reports the time-aggregated bound of $-1.20$,
which relaxes the one-period assumption in the way most
favourable to rational expectations.
The estimated coefficient exceeds every bound by a wide margin.

Because the CFPS question elicits direction rather than
magnitude, the ordinal coding $\mu \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$ is a
categorical indicator, not a point forecast.
Two features of the test are robust to this limitation.

The \emph{sign} of $\hat{\beta}_3 < 0$ is invariant to
any monotone rescaling of the ordinal variable.
The negative sign follows from two empirical facts established
separately: expectations respond positively to the shock
($\hat{\beta}_1 > 0$) while realised CPI responds negatively
($\hat{\beta}_2 < 0$).
Any mapping that preserves the ordering of ``fall,'' ``same,''
and ``rise'' produces a negative $\hat{\beta}_3$
(Appendix~\ref{app:scaling}).

The \emph{magnitude} comparison with the rational bound
does not require the ordinal variable at all.
The pass-through coefficient $\hat{\beta}_2 = -5.25$ is
measured entirely in CPI percentage points.
Setting the expectation scaling to zero (ignoring household
expectations) yields a forecast-error coefficient equal to
$\hat{\beta}_2$, which already exceeds the time-aggregated
rational bound of 1.20 by a factor of four.
The ordinal expectation adds further overreaction on top;
the exact amount depends on the coding, but the scaling-free
lower bound is decisive.
Appendix~\ref{app:scaling} reports results under three
alternative mappings and a Carlson-Parkin quantification that
converts ordinal responses to cardinal units.
All confirm the finding.

\subsection{Visualising the Treatment Variation}%
\label{sec:event_study}
% -------------------------------------------------------------

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.84\textwidth]{fig_meat_cpi_spaghetti.pdf}
\caption{Province-level meat CPI trajectories, 2016--2025.
  Each line is one province; colour indicates macro-region.
  The vertical dashed line marks August 2018 (first ASF case).
  The sharp divergence in 2019--2020 followed by mean reversion
  illustrates the temporary supply-shock nature of the ASF
  episode.}
\label{fig:meat_cpi_spaghetti}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:meat_cpi_spaghetti} displays province-level
meat CPI trajectories from 2016 to 2025.
All 31 provinces track closely before August 2018, then diverge
sharply, with some experiencing increases of 80--100 log points
and others 40--60 log points.
The trajectories converge back by 2021--2022 as herds recovered,
confirming the temporary nature of the shock.
The dispersion appears across regions, alleviating the
concern that results are driven by outliers.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{fig_meat_shock_distribution.pdf}
\caption{Distribution of province-level meat-price shocks
  by CFPS wave.  Each dot is one province; boxes show
  interquartile range.  The 2020 wave captures the ASF
  peak; the 2022 wave shows the reversal.}
\label{fig:meat_shock_dist}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:meat_shock_dist} shows the distribution of
the treatment variable by CFPS wave.
The 2020 wave has the largest and most dispersed shocks,
with an interquartile range of roughly 0.15 to 0.55 log points.
The 2022 wave shows negative shocks as meat prices reversed,
nearly uniformly across provinces.
Pre-ASF waves (2016, 2018) show modest shocks clustered
near zero, confirming that the ASF episode is the dominant
source of treatment variation.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{fig_meat_vs_headline.pdf}
\caption{Meat shock vs.\ forward realised headline CPI
  at the province level.
  The fitted line shows the negative relationship: provinces
  with larger meat shocks do not experience higher subsequent
  headline inflation.}
\label{fig:meat_vs_headline}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:meat_vs_headline} plots the meat shock against
forward headline CPI at the province level.
Each point is one province-wave cell.
The fitted line slopes downward, confirming that high-shock
provinces experienced lower subsequent headline inflation.
This provides a visual counterpart to
Table~\ref{tab:eq2_cpi_passthrough}.
The negative slope is the visual expression of the overreaction
logic: households raised expectations in response to a signal
that predicted the opposite of what they expected.
