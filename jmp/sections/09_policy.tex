\section{Policy Counterfactual and Backtest}\label{sec:policy}

\subsection{Forecast-adjustment rule}

The baseline estimates translate directly into a forecast-adjustment rule.
Let $\hat{FE}_{t+1}$ denote the model-implied forecast error:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:policy_rule}
  \hat{FE}_{t+1} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 FR_t
    + \hat{\gamma}' W_t.
\end{equation}
The adjusted forecast corrects the raw survey expectation by adding
the estimated error:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adjusted_forecast}
  \hat{\pi}_{t+1}^{adj} = \mu_t + \hat{FE}_{t+1}.
\end{equation}
When $\hat{\beta}_1 < 0$ and $FR_t > 0$, the adjustment lowers the
forecast relative to the survey expectation, reflecting the prediction
that realized inflation will undershoot the inflated belief.
Table~\ref{tab:policy_rule_formula} reports the mapping from estimated
coefficients to rule inputs. The adjustment requires no free parameters
beyond those already estimated and no structural model of the inflation
process; a central bank observing survey expectations and the same
macro controls can compute it in real time.

\subsection{Rolling backtest design}

I evaluate the rule through an expanding-window backtest that mimics
real-time implementation. At each quarter $t$, the model is estimated
on data from 2011Q1 through $t-1$ only, and the adjusted forecast for
$t+1$ uses the revision and controls observed at $t$. The naive
benchmark is the raw Carlson--Parkin expectation $\mu_t$. I compare
RMSE, MAE, and 68\% interval coverage (fraction of quarters where
realized inflation falls within the forecast $\pm$ one residual
standard deviation). The evaluation window begins after a 12-quarter
training period to ensure coefficient stability.

\subsection{Backtest results}

Table~\ref{tab:policy_backtest} reports performance over 12 evaluation
quarters. The results are mixed. The diagnostic-adjusted forecast
reduces MAE by 17\% (3.58 to 3.41), and 68\% interval coverage nearly
doubles (42\% to 67\%), meaning uncertainty bands contain realized
inflation in two-thirds of quarters rather than fewer than half. These
gains reflect the rule's ability to pull forecasts toward realizations
in quarters with large revisions.

RMSE, however, increases from 4.12 to 4.52. The deterioration traces
to the COVID-period outlier in 2020Q1, where both forecasts miss by a
large margin but the diagnostic correction amplifies the squared error.
The rule improves the typical forecast but worsens the worst miss.

\subsection{Limitations}

The backtest provides suggestive evidence but has clear limitations.
The evaluation window spans only 12 quarters, so the MAE improvement
could reflect parameter stability rather than genuine forecasting
skill. I do not report a formal Diebold--Mariano test because the
short evaluation sample yields insufficient power to distinguish
the two forecast paths statistically. The rule also uses aggregate
survey data and cannot incorporate household-level heterogeneity from
CFPS, limiting its precision when the composition of over-reactors
shifts.

More fundamentally, the backtest evaluates point-forecast accuracy
and interval coverage, not the welfare consequences of acting on the
adjusted forecast. A central bank using the rule to calibrate
communication faces a Lucas critique concern: if communication
successfully dampens diagnostic behavior, the estimated coefficients
shift and the rule requires re-estimation. The rule is therefore best
interpreted as a diagnostic warning system---flagging quarters when
survey expectations most likely overstate near-term inflation---rather
than a permanent forecasting model.
