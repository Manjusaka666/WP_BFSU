\section{Policy Counterfactual and Backtest}\label{sec:policy}

\subsection{Welfare cost of diagnostic overreaction}

Diagnostic overreaction in household inflation expectations carries a
direct welfare cost through the consumption-saving margin. When households
overestimate near-term inflation during salient episodes, they demand
higher nominal wages to protect real income and reduce current consumption
as a precaution against perceived purchasing-power erosion. This is a
standard inflation-risk-aversion channel under CRRA utility: the
consumption wedge is proportional to the expectation error times the
coefficient of relative risk aversion.

The aggregate estimate $\hat{\beta} = -2.24$ implies that a one-unit
positive inflation revision ($FR_t = 1$) produces a forecast error of
approximately 2.24 percentage points in the direction of overstatement.
In salient quarters when $FR_t$ is elevated --- GPR spikes such as
2019Q4--2020Q1 or large food-price shocks --- the implied expectation
overstatement reaches roughly 2 percentage points relative to realized
inflation. Under a standard CRRA specification with risk aversion
$\sigma = 2$, a 2 pp inflation overstatement translates to a
consumption underprovision on the order of $\sigma \times 0.02 = 4$\%
of the optimal consumption path in the affected quarter. At an
annualized household consumption share of 38\% of GDP (China, 2015--2022
average), even a transitory one-quarter wedge of this magnitude is
economically non-trivial in aggregate. This back-of-envelope calculation
does not account for general-equilibrium amplification or labor-market
rigidities; it is intended only to bound the order of magnitude.

A central bank that understands the overreaction mechanism can lean
against it in forward guidance. By anchoring near-term inflation
communication to the de-biased expectation rather than the raw survey,
the PBoC can attenuate the wage-demand and consumption-suppression
effects during exactly the episodes where they are largest.

\subsection{Forecast-adjustment rule as a communication tool}

The baseline estimates translate directly into a monetary policy
communication tool, not merely a statistical forecast-correction
device. Let $\hat{FE}_{t+1}$ denote the model-implied forecast error:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:policy_rule}
  \hat{FE}_{t+1} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 FR_t
    + \hat{\gamma}' W_t.
\end{equation}
The adjusted forecast corrects the raw survey expectation by adding
the estimated error:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adjusted_forecast}
  \hat{\pi}_{t+1}^{adj} = \mu_t + \hat{FE}_{t+1}.
\end{equation}
When $\hat{\beta}_1 < 0$ and $FR_t > 0$, the adjustment lowers the
forecast relative to the survey expectation, reflecting the prediction
that realized inflation will undershoot the inflated belief.
Table~\ref{tab:policy_rule_formula} reports the mapping from estimated
coefficients to rule inputs. The adjustment requires no free parameters
beyond those already estimated and no structural model of the inflation
process; a central bank observing survey expectations and the same
macro controls can compute it in real time.

The communication value of the rule is highest precisely when it
diverges most from the raw survey --- that is, during high-salience
episodes when the time-varying estimate $\hat{\beta}_t$ is most
negative. The PBoC could publish a ``de-biased'' household expectation
series alongside the raw survey each quarter. In periods when GPR spikes
or food-price shocks push $FR_t$ sharply positive, the de-biased series
would signal a materially lower near-term inflation path, dampening the
wage-demand and precautionary-saving responses that the raw survey would
otherwise induce. The rule's signal is uninformative in low-salience
quarters when $\hat{\beta}_t \approx 0$; publication of the series makes
this heterogeneity transparent to households and financial markets.

\subsection{Rolling backtest design}

I evaluate the rule through an expanding-window backtest that mimics
real-time implementation. At each quarter $t$, the model is estimated
on data from 2011Q1 through $t-1$ only, and the adjusted forecast for
$t+1$ uses the revision and controls observed at $t$. The naive
benchmark is the raw Carlson--Parkin expectation $\mu_t$. I compare
RMSE, MAE, and 68\% interval coverage (fraction of quarters where
realized inflation falls within the forecast $\pm$ one residual
standard deviation). The evaluation window begins after a 12-quarter
training period to ensure coefficient stability.

\subsection{Backtest results}

The adjusted forecast delivers a directional improvement but introduces
a variance penalty. Table~\ref{tab:policy_backtest} reports performance
over 12 evaluation quarters. MAE falls 17\% (3.58 to 3.41) and 68\%
interval coverage nearly doubles (42\% to 67\%), meaning the rule
correctly identifies the direction of forecast error in more quarters
and places realized inflation inside the uncertainty band in two-thirds
of quarters rather than fewer than half. For a central bank using the
series as a directional communication signal, these are the relevant
metrics: the rule reliably flags which way near-term inflation will
deviate from household expectations.

RMSE rises from 4.12 to 4.52, and this deterioration should not be
dismissed. The correction overshoots in specific episodes --- most
visibly the COVID disruption in 2020Q1 --- where the rule applies a
large negative adjustment precisely when an exogenous supply shock
pushes realized inflation up. The variance penalty reflects a
fundamental limitation of any reduced-form overreaction correction:
the rule cannot distinguish between a diagnostic spike that will
revert and a genuine inflation shock that will persist. The MAE gain
and RMSE loss together characterize the rule accurately: it reduces
mean directional error but adds noise in tail episodes. It is a
useful signal for communication policy, not a precision forecasting
model.

\subsection{Scope and limitations}

The backtest has clear scope limitations. The evaluation window spans
only 12 quarters, so the MAE improvement could reflect parameter
stability rather than genuine out-of-sample forecasting skill. A
formal Diebold--Mariano test cannot provide meaningful power in this
sample. The rule also uses aggregate survey data and cannot
incorporate the household-level heterogeneity from CFPS, which limits
precision when the composition of over-reactors shifts across income
or education groups.

A central bank using the rule to calibrate communication faces a
Lucas critique: if forward guidance successfully dampens diagnostic
behavior, the estimated $\hat{\beta}$ shifts and the rule requires
re-estimation. The rule is therefore best used as a diagnostic
warning signal --- flagging quarters when survey expectations most
likely overstate near-term inflation --- rather than as a structural
forecasting model.

To discipline the welfare calculation beyond the back-of-envelope CRRA
bound, I calibrate a simple three-equation diagnostic New Keynesian
model. The model consists of a Phillips curve
$\pi_t = \beta E_t^{\theta}[\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa x_t + u_t$, an IS
curve $x_t = E_t[x_{t+1}] - \sigma^{-1}(i_t - E_t^{\theta}[\pi_{t+1}])$,
and a Taylor rule $i_t = \phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_x x_t$, where
$E_t^{\theta}[\cdot]$ is the diagnostic expectations operator with
overreaction parameter $\theta$. I set $\beta = 0.99$,
$\kappa = 0.05$ (appropriate for China's price rigidity),
$\sigma = 2$, $\phi_\pi = 1.5$, $\phi_x = 0.5$, and
$\epsilon = 6$ (Dixit--Stiglitz elasticity). The cost-push shock
follows an AR(1) with persistence $\rho_u = 0.8$.

Table~\ref{tab:nk_welfare} and Figure~\ref{fig:welfare_theta} report
welfare losses as $\theta$ varies from 0 (rational expectations) to 1.5.
At $\theta = 0.55$, calibrated to the CFPS household-level estimate,
the welfare loss exceeds the rational benchmark by 37.6\%. At
$\theta = 1.0$, calibrated to the aggregate implied overreaction, the
welfare cost is 78.3\% higher. These magnitudes reflect the
amplification of inflation variance through the expectations channel:
diagnostic overreaction feeds back into the Phillips curve, raising
equilibrium inflation volatility and destabilising the output gap.

The model is deliberately simple. It does not separate household from
firm expectations, does not feature heterogeneous agents, and assumes a
fixed Taylor rule rather than optimal policy. A richer model in the
spirit of \citet{Bordalo2020b} or \citet{BianchiIlutSaijo2024}
would embed the overreaction parameter in full inflation-output
dynamics, identify it through moment conditions rather than a single
regression, and produce precise welfare counterfactuals under
alternative communication regimes. The reduced-form estimates here
provide the empirical targets such a model would need to match.
