\section{Forecasting Implications}\label{sec:policy}

The documented overreaction has direct forecasting implications.
Survey revisions predict subsequent forecast errors with a stable
negative sign, so a simple correction can reduce predictable error in
real time. This section presents the correction rule, evaluates it in
an expanding-window backtest, and states the limits of the exercise.

\subsection{Forecast Correction Rule}

The correction is a one-step linear adjustment built from the estimated
revision-error relationship. Let $FR_t$ denote the quarter-$t$
inflation forecast revision and let $W_t$ collect controls observed at
$t$ (food CPI, M2 growth, and PPI). The predicted forecast error is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:policy_rule_policy}
  \widehat{FE}_{t+1 \mid t}
  = \hat{\beta}_{0,t} + \hat{\beta}_{1,t} FR_t + \hat{\gamma}_t' W_t .
\end{equation}
The adjusted inflation forecast is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:adjusted_forecast_policy}
  \widehat{\pi}^{adj}_{t+1 \mid t}
  = \mu_t + \widehat{FE}_{t+1 \mid t},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_t$ is the raw Carlson-Parkin survey forecast. Under the
estimated sign $\hat{\beta}_{1,t} < 0$, a positive revision implies
subsequent overprediction, so the correction moves the forecast
downward.

All inputs are available at decision time. At each quarter $t$, I
observe the current survey forecast, the current revision, and
contemporaneous macro controls. Coefficients are re-estimated using
only information through $t-1$, then frozen for the $t+1$ forecast.
No latent state extraction, no future-information filtering, and no
discretionary tuning is needed after model selection.

Table~\ref{tab:policy_rule_formula} reports the mapping from estimated
coefficients to rule inputs. The revision slope is negative and
economically material: larger upward revisions predict larger negative
errors next quarter. Because the rule is linear, the adjustment
decomposes into an intercept term, a revision term, and control terms.

This is a reduced-form forecasting tool, not a structural policy
prescription. If a quarter is dominated by a persistent supply shock
that the revision variable does not capture, the adjustment can misfire.
The relevant benchmark is whether the rule improves forecast performance
relative to the unadjusted survey under genuinely real-time conditions.

\subsection{Backtest}

I evaluate the rule with an expanding-window backtest over 12
forecast quarters. The first estimation window begins in 2011Q1 and
expands recursively. For each forecast origin $t$, coefficients are
estimated using data through $t-1$, then used to generate the one-step
adjusted forecast for $t+1$. The benchmark is the raw survey forecast
$\mu_t$.

Three metrics summarize performance: RMSE, MAE, and interval coverage.
Coverage is the share of quarters in which realized inflation falls
inside a model-implied uncertainty band around the point forecast.
With only 12 backtest observations, the exercise cannot deliver
high-power statistical tests. Its purpose is to check whether the
correction improves forecast quality in a sample that includes
high-salience episodes.

The results show mixed performance. MAE declines by 17\%, from 3.58 to
3.41, and 68\% coverage rises from 42\% to 67\%. These gains suggest
that the correction can improve central tendency and uncertainty
calibration for most quarters. At the same time, RMSE rises from 4.12
to 4.52, reflecting the bias-variance tradeoff inherent in the
adjustment. The increase is concentrated in tail episodes where the
correction is large but the subsequent inflation shock does not
mean-revert as quickly as the rule expects. In those cases the
squared-error penalty dominates the absolute error gain.

The correction improves directional accuracy in most quarters but does
not universally reduce forecast variance. A 12-quarter backtest with
this pattern supports a limited claim: the overreaction signal contains
usable forecasting information, but the correction is not a universal
variance-minimizing tool.

Figure~\ref{fig:policy_backtest_policy} shows where the gains come from. The
adjusted path tracks reversals more closely after large positive survey
revisions, reducing systematic overprediction. It performs less well in
quarters dominated by persistent exogenous shocks. This pattern is
consistent with the mechanism estimates: the correction removes a
behavioral overreaction component but does not substitute for a
structural shock decomposition.
