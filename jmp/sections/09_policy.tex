\section{Policy Implications}\label{sec:policy}

This section translates the empirical result into a policy object that
can be implemented with observed data at decision time. The objective
is narrow: improve near-term inflation guidance when survey revisions
are likely to reflect overreaction. I then evaluate the rule in a
real-time backtest and quantify welfare implications in a compact
New Keynesian benchmark.

\subsection{Forecast Rule}

The policy object is a one-step forecast correction built from the
estimated revision--error relationship. Let $FR_t$ denote the quarter-$t$
inflation forecast revision and let $W_t$ collect controls observed at
$t$ (food CPI, M2 growth, and PPI). The predicted forecast error is
\begin{equation}\label{policy_rule}
  \widehat{FE}_{t+1 \mid t}
  = \hat{\beta}_{0,t} + \hat{\beta}_{1,t} FR_t + \hat{\gamma}_t' W_t .
\end{equation}
The adjusted inflation forecast is
\begin{equation}\label{adjusted_forecast}
  \widehat{\pi}^{adj}_{t+1 \mid t}
  = \mu_t + \widehat{FE}_{t+1 \mid t},
\end{equation}
where $\mu_t$ is the raw Carlson--Parkin survey forecast. Under the
estimated sign $\hat{\beta}_{1,t} < 0$, a positive revision implies
subsequent overprediction of realized inflation, so the correction
moves the forecast downward.

The rule is real-time implementable. At each quarter $t$, all required
inputs are available without look-ahead: the current survey forecast,
the current revision, and contemporaneous macro controls. Coefficients
are re-estimated using only information through $t-1$, then frozen for
the $t+1$ forecast. No latent state extraction, no future-information
filtering, and no discretionary tuning parameter is needed after model
selection. In operational terms, this is a transparent add-on to the
existing survey monitoring process rather than a replacement for the
central bank's structural projection framework.

Table~\ref{tab:policy_rule_formula} reports the mapping from estimated
coefficients to rule inputs. The revision slope is negative and
economically material: larger upward revisions predict larger negative
errors next quarter. Because the rule is linear, policy communication
can disclose the adjustment as a decomposition into an intercept term,
a revision term, and control terms. That decomposition is useful when
the central bank needs to explain why the de-biased path differs from
the raw survey in a specific quarter.

The interpretation is disciplined. The correction is designed to reduce
predictable survey error, not to infer the full structural inflation
process. If a quarter is dominated by a persistent supply shock that the
revision variable does not capture, the adjustment can misfire. This is
a feature of any reduced-form correction rule. The relevant benchmark is
whether the rule improves directional and absolute forecast performance
relative to the unadjusted survey in genuinely real-time conditions.

\subsection{Backtest}

I evaluate the rule with an expanding-window backtest over 12
forecast quarters. The first estimation window begins in 2011Q1 and
expands recursively. For each forecast origin $t$, coefficients are
estimated using data through $t-1$, then used to generate the one-step
adjusted forecast for $t+1$. This design mirrors real-time policy use:
historical coefficients are available, future realizations are not.
The benchmark is the raw survey forecast $\mu_t$.

Three metrics summarize performance: RMSE, MAE, and interval coverage.
Coverage is the share of quarters in which realized inflation falls
inside a model-implied uncertainty band around the point forecast.
With only 12 backtest observations, the exercise is not intended to
deliver high-power statistical tests. Its purpose is to establish
whether the correction improves operational forecast quality in a sample
that includes high-salience episodes.

Results show a clear tradeoff. MAE declines by 17\%, from 3.58 to 3.41,
and 68\% coverage rises from 42\% to 67\%. These gains indicate that the
rule improves central tendency and uncertainty calibration for most
quarters. The improvement is economically meaningful for communication
policy: fewer large directional misses and a higher probability that
realized inflation lands inside the published confidence band.

At the same time, RMSE rises from 4.12 to 4.52, reflecting the bias-variance tradeoff inherent in the adjustment. The increase is
concentrated in tail episodes where the correction is large but the
subsequent inflation shock does not mean-revert as quickly as the rule
expects. In those cases the squared-error penalty dominates the absolute
error gain. The full backtest therefore supports a constrained claim:
the rule is useful as a communication-oriented de-biasing device, but it
is not a universal variance-minimizing forecaster.

overreaction component but does not substitute for a structural shock
decomposition.

\subsection{NK Welfare}

To quantify welfare implications beyond forecast metrics, I use a
three-equation New Keynesian benchmark with diagnostic expectations.
The model is
\begin{align}
  \pi_t &= \beta E_t^\theta[\pi_{t+1}] + \kappa x_t + u_t, \nonumber \\
  x_t &= E_t[x_{t+1}] - \sigma^{-1}
    \left(i_t - E_t^\theta[\pi_{t+1}]\right), \nonumber \\
  i_t &= \phi_\pi \pi_t + \phi_x x_t. \nonumber
\end{align}
Calibration follows the baseline values used in the quantitative
exercise: $\beta = 0.99$, $\kappa = 0.05$, and $\sigma = 2$ (with
$\phi_\pi = 1.5$, $\phi_x = 0.5$, and AR(1) cost-push shocks). The
parameter $\theta$ scales expectation overreaction: $\theta = 0$ is
rational expectations and larger $\theta$ implies stronger diagnostic
distortion.

Two calibration points are central. At $\theta = 0.55$, aligned with the
household estimate, welfare loss is 37.6\% above the rational benchmark.
At $\theta = 1.0$, aligned with the aggregate implied distortion, welfare
loss is 78.3\% above benchmark. These are large increases in policy loss
for parameter values supported by the data. The mechanism is standard:
diagnostic expectations amplify inflation and output-gap volatility, and
the welfare function penalizes both.

Table~\ref{tab:nk_welfare} and Figure~\ref{fig:welfare_theta} show that
the welfare schedule is convex in $\theta$ over the relevant range. This
convexity matters for policy design. A partial reduction in
overreaction can yield first-order gains when the economy operates in a
high-$\theta$ region, even if communication policy cannot fully eliminate
behavioral distortions.

The welfare exercise should be interpreted with one key caveat: Lucas
critique. If policy communication changes household belief formation, the
reduced-form mapping from revisions to errors and the implied $\theta$
can shift endogenously. The reported numbers are therefore local
counterfactuals around the estimated regime, not invariant policy
constants. This limitation does not invalidate the exercise; it defines
its scope. The model is informative about the order of magnitude of
welfare gains from lowering diagnostic distortion, while a full policy
evaluation would require a model in which belief dynamics adjust to the
communication rule itself.
