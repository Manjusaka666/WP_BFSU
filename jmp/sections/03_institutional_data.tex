\section{Institutional Setting and Data}\label{sec:data}

This paper combines two complementary survey sources with
quarterly macroeconomic data to test for diagnostic
expectations at both aggregate and household levels. The
PBoC Urban Depositor Survey provides a long quarterly
time series of aggregate expectation shares; the China
Family Panel Studies (CFPS) provides a rich household panel
with demographic and financial detail. Together they allow
identification of overreaction in the aggregate, exploration
of heterogeneity across households, and validation of the
quantification procedure.

\subsection{PBoC Urban Depositor Survey}

The People's Bank of China has conducted the Urban
Depositor Survey (城镇储户问卷调查) quarterly since 1999,
sampling approximately 20,000 respondents across 50 cities
each wave. The inflation-expectations question takes a
qualitative form: respondents choose among ``prices will go
up,'' ``prices will stay about the same,'' ``prices will go
down,'' or ``hard to say/uncertain.''

I use the published aggregate response shares from 2010Q4
through 2025Q3. Table~\ref{tab:sample_overview} reports
the estimation window and usable observations after
aligning survey timing with realized inflation outcomes.
The survey is fielded in the last month of each quarter,
so expectation $\mu_t$ is measured at time $t$ and the
forecast error $FE_{t+1} = \pi_{t+1} - \mu_t$ uses
realized inflation from the subsequent quarter.

Two features make this survey well suited for testing
diagnostic expectations. First, the quarterly frequency
yields enough time-series observations ($T = 57$) to
estimate a time-varying diagnostic coefficient with the
state-space model in Section~\ref{sec:measurement}.
Second, the qualitative format reduces cognitive burden
relative to point-estimate elicitation, increasing response
quality for a population with limited financial literacy.
The cost is that mapping qualitative shares into a
quantitative expectation requires threshold assumptions,
which I address in Section~\ref{sec:measurement}.

\subsection{China Family Panel Studies (CFPS)}

The CFPS is a nationally representative longitudinal
household survey administered by the Institute of Social
Science Survey at Peking University \citep{Xie2014,
Xie2022}. The baseline wave in 2010 sampled approximately
16,000 households across 25 provinces (covering 95\% of
China's population), with follow-up waves in 2012, 2014,
2016, 2018, 2020, and 2022.

The CFPS adult questionnaire asks respondents whether
local commodity prices will rise, stay the same, or fall
over the next year. This qualitative format mirrors the
PBoC survey, so I apply the same Carlson--Parkin
methodology to individual responses rather than aggregate
shares. The result is a household-level expectation
measure that can be matched to each respondent's
demographics, income, and financial holdings.

Three properties distinguish the CFPS from the PBoC
aggregate data. First, the panel structure tracks the same
households over seven waves, enabling within-household
tests of how expectation errors predict consumption and
savings adjustments. Second, the rich covariate set
(education, age, hukou status, income, asset holdings)
supports heterogeneity analysis that the aggregate series
cannot deliver. Third, comparing individual-level
Carlson--Parkin estimates against the aggregate PBoC
measure provides external validation of the quantification
procedure.

Table~\ref{tab:cfps_summary} reports sample sizes by wave
after dropping observations with missing price-expectation
responses and applying the household panel identifier for
tracking across waves. The attrition rate is approximately
15\% per wave, concentrated among migrant households and
those in rapidly urbanizing areas, which I address through
inverse probability weighting in the heterogeneity
analysis.

\subsection{Macroeconomic Controls and Uncertainty Indices}

I merge survey data with quarterly macroeconomic series
from National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) releases and
standard uncertainty indices.

\paragraph{Inflation measures.}
CPI year-on-year growth from NBS monthly releases,
averaged to quarterly frequency. I construct both
year-on-year and quarter-on-quarter annualized measures;
the latter maps more directly to the PBoC survey's
quarterly horizon. Food CPI enters separately because
food-price dynamics in China follow different cycles than
core inflation. PPI year-on-year captures upstream price
pressure that may reach households through news coverage
even when direct CPI transmission is slow.

\paragraph{Monetary and real-activity indicators.}
M2 year-on-year growth proxies the monetary stance.
Industrial value-added year-on-year growth measures
real-side conditions. Both enter control sets because
households may form inflation expectations partly through
real-activity signals and monetary-policy narratives.

\paragraph{Uncertainty indices.}
The Economic Policy Uncertainty index for China
\citep{Baker2016} captures newspaper-based policy
uncertainty at monthly frequency, averaged to quarters.
The Geopolitical Risk index \citep{Caldara2022} measures
global geopolitical threats from automated text analysis
of major news sources. Both enter as state variables in
heterogeneity interactions and as components of the
salience-IV design.

Table~\ref{tab:desc_stats} reports descriptive statistics
for all variables in the estimation sample. The mean
forecast error is $+1.32$ percentage points (signed mean,
not mean absolute error), indicating that households on
average underestimate inflation. This
level bias is distinct from the diagnostic overreaction
pattern, which concerns the covariance between revisions
and errors rather than the error level.

\subsection{Geographic Coverage and Data Timeline}

Figure~\ref{fig:china_cfps_map} maps the two data sources.
The 25 CFPS provinces cover the densely populated coastal
and central regions; the 50 PBoC cities concentrate in the
same geography. The spatial overlap ensures that aggregate
and household-level analyses describe the same expectation
environment.

Figure~\ref{fig:expectations_vs_realized} plots quantified
expectations against realized inflation over the sample
period. The expectation series is smoother than realized
inflation, consistent with partial updating. Gaps widen
during high-uncertainty episodes, and expectations
overstate inflation on average, a level bias captured by
the intercept in the measurement equation.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.90\textwidth]{revision_error_dynamics.pdf}
\caption{Forecast revision (bars, left axis) and subsequent
  one-quarter-ahead forecast error (line, right axis),
  2011Q1--2025Q3. Shaded episodes mark quarters where a
  large positive revision preceded a negative forecast
  error---the visible signature of the revision--error
  covariance that Section~\ref{sec:results} estimates
  formally.}
\label{fig:revision_error_dynamics}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:revision_error_dynamics} shows the raw
revision--error dynamics that motivate the formal
identification. Quarters with large positive revisions are
followed by negative forecast errors, the signature of
diagnostic overreaction. The pattern is visible in the raw
data but requires formal testing because macro controls,
salience variation, and timing structure could generate the
same covariance through other channels.
