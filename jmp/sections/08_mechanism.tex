\section{Mechanism Competition}\label{sec:mechanism}

This section runs a direct mechanism competition between
forecast-revision overreaction and information rigidity.
The evidence is informative but not over-claimed. The
joint specification is useful as a diagnostic screen,
not as a fully clean decomposition of structural channels.
The key result is that the lagged-error term is negative
and significant in the horse race, which implies error
reversals rather than sluggish persistence.
That pattern is inconsistent with a canonical rigidity
interpretation and consistent with a diagnostic
reversal dynamic.

\subsection{Horse Race}\label{subsec:horse_race}

I estimate a joint specification that nests the two
competing channels:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:horse_race}
  FE_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 FR_t
    + \lambda FE_t + \gamma' W_t + \varepsilon_t,
\end{equation}
where $FR_t$ is the forecast revision,
$FE_t$ is the lagged forecast error,
and $W_t$ contains macro controls.
Under diagnostic overreaction, $\beta_1<0$ because larger
upward revisions should be followed by negative errors.
Under information rigidity, $\lambda>0$ because past
errors should persist when updating is slow.

The horse-race estimates deliver two facts.
First, the revision coefficient attenuates once
$FE_t$ is included jointly.
Second, the lagged-error coefficient is
$\hat{\lambda}=-0.60$ with $p=0.005$.
The attenuation in $\hat{\beta}_1$ is expected in this
small sample because revisions and lagged errors both
load on the same overreaction process.
The revision coefficient attenuates to near zero in the joint specification (column~3), consistent with collinearity between revision and lagged error in a short sample.
The sign of $\hat{\lambda}$ is the decisive diagnostic:
it is negative, not positive.

A negative $\lambda$ means error reversals.
A positive forecast error in period $t$ predicts
a negative error in $t+1$.
That is the opposite of the persistence logic in
standard rigidity models, which require $\lambda>0$.
So the horse race does not support a rigidity reading
of the lagged-error channel.
Instead, it shows that the lagged-error regressor is
capturing correction after prior overshooting.

This is why I treat the horse race as a diagnostic test,
not a winner-take-all contest.
The joint regression does not prove that only one
mechanism exists.
It does show that the data reject a strict
rigidity interpretation of the lagged-error term,
because the estimated sign runs in the wrong direction.
In short: revision attenuation in the joint model is
not evidence for rigidity; the negative
$\hat{\lambda}$ points to diagnostic reversals.

\subsection{Time-Varying Coefficient}%
\label{subsec:mechanism_state}

The next question is whether diagnostic intensity is
state-dependent.
I first augment the horse race with an interaction:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mechanism_state}
  FE_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 FR_t
    + \beta_2 (FR_t \times D_t^{high})
    + \lambda FE_t + \gamma' W_t + \varepsilon_t,
\end{equation}
where $D_t^{high}$ marks high-uncertainty periods.
The estimate is $\hat{\beta}_2=-1.72$ with $p=0.357$.
The sign is in the diagnostic direction, but precision
is limited and the interaction is not statistically
distinguishable from zero.

I therefore rely on the full state-space evidence for
time variation.
Appendix B (Appendix~\ref{app:ssm}) estimates a Bayesian
SSM with a time-varying diagnostic coefficient $\beta_t$.
The estimated path becomes more negative during crisis
windows and relaxes toward zero in calmer periods.
The largest spikes line up with major stress episodes,
including geopolitical shocks and the pandemic onset.
This pattern is exactly what a salience-driven mechanism
would predict: overweighting strengthens when public
signals are vivid and uncertainty is elevated.

The state-variation evidence should still be read
carefully.
The interaction term in the reduced-form horse race is
imprecise, and the SSM is best viewed as dynamic
measurement rather than independent causal identification.
But taken together, the two pieces support a common
reading: diagnostic intensity is not constant; it moves
with macro-financial stress.

\subsection{Revision-Error Gradient}%
\label{subsec:mechanism_chain}

A separate nonparametric check bins quarters by revision
magnitude and computes average subsequent errors within
revision quintiles.
If revisions are diagnostic overreactions,
the gradient should be monotone: larger positive
revisions should map into more negative future errors.
That is what the data show.

The top revision quintile has
$\overline{FE}_{t+1}=-0.68$.
The bottom quintile is approximately zero.
Intermediate bins move monotonically between those
endpoints.
This shape is hard to reconcile with rational
expectations, which imply errors centered near zero in
every bin, and with pure rigidity, which predicts
persistence in past errors rather than a monotone
mapping from current revisions to next-period errors.

\subsection{CHFS Channel Test}\label{subsec:chfs_info_channel}

The CHFS test asks a narrower question:
does a household's persistent information channel
explain cross-sectional variation in inflation beliefs?
If a channel-driven mechanism were central,
households using high-salience media should show
systematically stronger inflation beliefs or more
extreme belief errors.
If the mechanism is event-triggered rather than
channel-driven, these cross-sectional differences can be
small or null once location controls are included.

The baseline CHFS equation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chfs_info_channel}
  Exp_{ip} = \alpha + \beta_1 HighMedia_i
    + \gamma X_i + \delta_p + \varepsilon_{ip},
\end{equation}
where $Exp_{ip}$ is the household inflation belief,
$HighMedia_i$ captures intensive use of TV/internet/news,
$X_i$ contains household covariates,
and $\delta_p$ are province fixed effects.

The result is null.
In the main specification,
$\hat{\beta}_1=-0.005$ (SE $=0.022$).
The point estimate is essentially zero and precisely
enough to rule out economically large channel effects.
Figure~\ref{fig:chfs_info_channel} shows the same pattern
visually: high-media and low-media households have very
similar mean beliefs after accounting for province-level
composition.

A risk-attitude placebo is also null:
$\hat{\beta}=-0.010$ (SE $=0.008$).
This matters because it reduces the concern that the
CHFS mechanism coefficients are being driven by a broad
latent trait such as optimism, pessimism, or generalized
risk tolerance rather than a specific information process.

The CHFS evidence should be stated plainly.
It does not support a channel-driven mechanism.
Combined with the aggregate and state-varying results,
the most credible interpretation is that overreaction is
activated by salient events, not by permanent differences
in media exposure.
So this section rules out the channel-driven version of
the mechanism while leaving the event-driven diagnostic
channel intact.
