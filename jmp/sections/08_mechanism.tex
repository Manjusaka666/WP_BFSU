\section{Dynamic Interpretation: Overreaction versus Persistence}%
\label{sec:interpretation} % formerly \label{sec:mechanism}

The baseline results establish that forecast revisions
predict future errors with a negative sign.
This section asks whether that pattern reflects behavioral
overreaction or whether it could instead arise from
information rigidity or other forms of expectation
persistence.
I do not claim to fully distinguish diagnostic
overreaction from every alternative dynamic process.
What I can show is that the sign and timing of forecast
errors align with reversal rather than with monotone
persistence.
The evidence is therefore more consistent with an
overreaction account than with a pure rigidity
interpretation.

\subsection{Nesting Overreaction and Persistence}%
\label{sec:horse_race}

I estimate a joint specification that nests both channels:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:horse_race}
  FE_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 FR_t
    + \lambda FE_t + \gamma' W_t + \varepsilon_t,
\end{equation}
where $FR_t$ is the forecast revision,
$FE_t$ is the lagged forecast error,
and $W_t$ contains macro controls.
Under overreaction, $\beta_1<0$ because larger
upward revisions should be followed by negative errors.
Under information rigidity, $\lambda>0$ because past
errors should persist when updating is slow.

Two results stand out.
First, the revision coefficient flips sign, attenuates to
near zero, and loses precision in the joint specification
(column 3), consistent with collinearity between revision
and lagged error in a short sample.
Given the limited sample, this sign flip should be read as
an imprecise joint-specification estimate rather than as a
stable change in the underlying behavioral relationship.

Second, the lagged-error coefficient is
$\hat{\lambda}=-0.60$ with $p=0.005$.
A negative $\lambda$ means that a positive forecast error
in period $t$ predicts a negative error in $t+1$.
That is error reversal, the opposite of the persistence
pattern that canonical rigidity models require
($\lambda>0$).

This result allows a limited but clear statement.
The data reject a strict rigidity interpretation of the
lagged-error term because the estimated sign runs in the
wrong direction.
The negative $\hat{\lambda}$ is consistent with correction
after prior overshooting, the signature of a reversal
dynamic.
At the same time, the joint regression is a diagnostic
screen, not a structural decomposition.
It does not rule out that both overreaction and some
degree of sluggish updating coexist.
It does show that the dominant pattern in the data is
reversal, not persistence.

\subsection{Dynamic Measurement of Overreaction Intensity}%
\label{sec:mechanism_state}

Is the degree of overreaction constant over time?
I augment the joint specification with an interaction:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mechanism_state}
  FE_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 FR_t
    + \beta_2 (FR_t \times D_t^{high})
    + \lambda FE_t + \gamma' W_t + \varepsilon_t,
\end{equation}
where $D_t^{high}$ marks high-uncertainty periods.
The estimate is $\hat{\beta}_2=-1.72$ with $p=0.357$.
The sign points toward stronger overreaction in volatile
periods, but precision is limited.
The interaction is not statistically distinguishable from
zero.

A Bayesian state-space model (Appendix~\ref{app:ssm})
provides a richer view.
It estimates a time-varying diagnostic coefficient
$\beta_t$ whose posterior path becomes more negative
during crisis windows and relaxes toward zero in calmer
periods.
The largest spikes coincide with major stress episodes,
including geopolitical shocks and the pandemic onset.
This pattern is what a salience-driven account would
predict: overweighting strengthens when public signals are
vivid and uncertainty is elevated.

I treat the SSM evidence as dynamic measurement, not as
independent causal identification.
The reduced-form interaction is imprecise, and the SSM
smooths over a short sample.
Together, the two exercises suggest that overreaction
intensity is not constant but moves with macro-financial
stress.
Neither exercise pins down the structural channel with
certainty.

\subsection{Revision-Error Gradient}%
\label{sec:mechanism_chain}

A nonparametric check bins quarters by revision magnitude
and computes average subsequent errors within revision
quintiles.
If revisions reflect overreaction, the gradient should be
monotone: larger positive revisions should map into more
negative future errors.

That is what the data show.
The top revision quintile has
$\overline{FE}_{t+1}=-0.68$.
The bottom quintile is approximately zero.
Intermediate bins move monotonically between those
endpoints.

This monotone shape is hard to reconcile with rational
expectations, which imply errors centered near zero in
every bin.
It is also hard to reconcile with pure rigidity, which
predicts persistence in past errors rather than a
systematic mapping from current revisions to next-period
errors.
The gradient therefore provides an independent check that
the reversal pattern in the joint regression is not an
artifact of functional form.

\subsection{CHFS Channel Test: A Boundary Condition}%
\label{sec:chfs_info_channel}

The CHFS test asks a narrower question:
does a household's persistent information channel explain
cross-sectional variation in inflation beliefs?
If a channel-driven mechanism were important, households
using high-salience media should show systematically
stronger inflation beliefs or more extreme belief errors.
The baseline equation is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chfs_info_channel}
  Exp_{ip} = \alpha + \beta_1 HighMedia_i
    + \gamma X_i + \delta_p + \varepsilon_{ip},
\end{equation}
where $Exp_{ip}$ is the household inflation belief,
$HighMedia_i$ captures intensive use of TV/internet/news,
$X_i$ contains household covariates,
and $\delta_p$ are province fixed effects.

The result is null.
In the main specification,
$\hat{\beta}_1=-0.005$ (SE $=0.022$).
The point estimate is essentially zero and precise enough
to rule out economically large channel effects.
Figure~\ref{fig:chfs_info_channel} shows the same pattern
visually: high-media and low-media households have very
similar mean beliefs after accounting for province-level
composition.

A risk-attitude placebo is also null:
$\hat{\beta}=-0.010$ (SE $=0.008$).
This reduces the concern that the mechanism coefficients
reflect a broad latent trait such as optimism or
generalized risk tolerance rather than a specific
information process.

This null result is a boundary condition, not a general
rejection of media amplification.
It means that persistent cross-sectional differences in
media exposure alone do not explain the expectation
pattern observed in the aggregate data.
The finding is consistent with the view that overreaction
is activated by salient events rather than by permanent
differences in information channels.
But the CHFS cross-section cannot speak to how media
coverage of specific episodes might amplify overreaction
in real time.
I therefore interpret the null as ruling out one version
of the channel story while leaving event-driven
amplification as an open question.
