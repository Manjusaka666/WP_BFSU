\section{Mechanism Competition}\label{sec:mechanism}

The horse-race specification in this section does not cleanly
discriminate between diagnostic overreaction and information
rigidity. When both channels enter jointly, the lagged-error
term absorbs the revision's explanatory power, but this
outcome is expected whenever the two regressors measure the
same underlying process. The specification tests whether
each variable carries independent predictive power. It cannot
assign that power to a causal mechanism. The key result is
that the lagged-error coefficient is strongly negative,
inconsistent with canonical information-rigidity dynamics
and consistent with the cross-period autocorrection that
diagnostic expectations predict. The analysis proceeds
from a joint regression to a state-dependent interaction,
then to the revision-error gradient in levels, and finally
to a micro-level CFPS test.

\subsection{Joint specification}\label{subsec:horse_race}

I nest the diagnostic and rigidity channels in a single
regression:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:horse_race}
  FE_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 FR_t
    + \lambda FE_t + \gamma' W_t + \varepsilon_t,
\end{equation}
where $FR_t$ is the forecast revision (diagnostic channel)
and $FE_t$ is the lagged forecast error (rigidity channel).
Diagnostic expectations predict $\beta_1 < 0$: larger
upward revisions cause larger negative subsequent errors.
Information rigidity predicts $\lambda > 0$: past errors
persist because agents update slowly.

Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_horse_race} reports three
specifications. In the diagnostic-only column,
$\hat{\beta}_1 = -2.24$ ($p = 0.055$). In the
rigidity-only column, $\hat{\lambda} = -0.58$
($p < 0.001$). In the joint specification,
$\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.25$ ($p = 0.88$) while
$\hat{\lambda} = -0.60$ ($p = 0.005$). The lagged-error
term absorbs the revision's explanatory power.

The critical observation is the sign of $\hat{\lambda}$.
Canonical information-rigidity models \citep{Coibion2015}
predict $\lambda > 0$: agents who update slowly carry
forward errors of the same sign. The strongly negative
$\hat{\lambda}$ instead implies error reversals, where a
positive error in quarter $t$ predicts a negative error in
$t+1$. Error reversals are a hallmark of overreaction, not
sluggish updating. The lagged-error channel thus captures
a diagnostic dynamic rather than a rigidity dynamic, and
its dominance in the joint regression reflects the fact
that $FE_t$ is a more efficient summary of past
overreaction than $FR_t$ alone.

Two limitations deserve note. First, multicollinearity between
$FR_t$ and $FE_t$ (correlation $= -0.22$) with $N = 32$
means the joint specification cannot assign explanatory power
to either channel independently; both carry information about
the same overreaction process. The revision coefficient's
sign flip in the joint model is a standard symptom of
collinear regressors, not evidence that revisions are
uninformative. Second, the negative $\hat{\lambda}$ is
consistent with diagnostic autocorrection but also with
other mean-reverting processes. The sign alone does not
identify the mechanism. What it rules out is canonical
information rigidity, which requires $\lambda > 0$.

\subsection{State-dependent interaction}%
\label{subsec:mechanism_state}

The diagnostic channel should strengthen when salient
signals dominate background information. Column~4 of
Table~\ref{tab:mechanism_horse_race} adds an interaction
with a high-uncertainty indicator:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mechanism_state}
  FE_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 FR_t
    + \beta_2 (FR_t \times D_t^{high})
    + \lambda FE_t + \gamma' W_t + \varepsilon_t.
\end{equation}

The interaction estimate $\hat{\beta}_2 = -1.72$
($p = 0.357$) is directionally consistent with
diagnosticity but imprecisely estimated. In
high-uncertainty states, the total revision effect is
$\hat{\beta}_1 + \hat{\beta}_2 = -0.27$, substantially
more negative than the base coefficient of $+1.45$, while
the lagged-error coefficient $\hat{\lambda} = -0.64$
($p = 0.01$) remains significant. The pattern accords
with representativeness operating through signal salience,
but the sample size prevents sharp inference on the
interaction alone.

The time-varying diagnostic coefficient $\hat{\beta}_t$
from the Bayesian state-space model provides complementary
evidence on state dependence. $\hat{\beta}_t$ intensifies
(becomes more negative) during geopolitical crises and
pandemic onset and relaxes toward zero in calmer periods.
This variation is consistent with the diagnostic channel
activating when salient signals are strongest, while the
lagged-error term carries residual persistence in low-salience
states. The two mechanisms coexist; their relative weight
shifts across macroeconomic states rather than one
displacing the other.

\subsection{The revision--error chain in levels}%
\label{subsec:mechanism_chain}

The monotone gradient from revisions to subsequent errors
is the defining signature of the diagnostic mechanism. I
bin quarters by revision magnitude and compute the average
subsequent forecast error within each bin.
Table~\ref{tab:revision_predicts_error} and
Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism_chain} display the result.

Quarters with the largest positive revisions (top
quintile) produce the most negative forecast errors
($\overline{FE} = -0.68$); quarters with negative
revisions produce errors near zero ($\overline{FE} =
+0.04$). The gradient is monotone across all bins.
Rational expectations imply errors centered on zero in
every bin. Pure rigidity implies no systematic gradient,
because it operates through persistence of past errors
rather than the direction of current revisions. Only
diagnostic overreaction predicts that the sign and
magnitude of the revision map onto the sign and magnitude
of the subsequent error.

\subsection{Micro-level mechanism test with CFPS}%
\label{subsec:mechanism_cfps}

Household-level data from the CFPS provide an independent
test. I classify households as ``revisers'' (those who
changed their inflation expectation between waves) or
``non-revisers,'' then compare realized forecast errors.
Under diagnostic expectations, revisers should make larger
errors because revising reflects exposure to a salient
signal that triggers overweighting. Under rigidity,
revisers should make smaller errors because they are the
faster updaters who close their information gap.

The CFPS data favor diagnosticity. Revisers exhibit
forecast-error magnitudes approximately 25\% larger than
non-revisers after controlling for province, wave, and
household demographics. Among revisers, upward movers show
negative subsequent errors (prices rose less than
expected), while downward movers show positive errors
(prices fell less than expected). This sign pattern at the
household level mirrors the aggregate quarterly gradient
and is difficult to reconcile with pure information
rigidity, where faster updaters should produce smaller
errors rather than larger ones.

\subsection{What would sharpen mechanism discrimination}%
\label{subsec:mechanism_future}

The current evidence is consistent with diagnostic
overreaction but cannot rule out all competing
interpretations. Three data structures would sharpen the
identification. First, eliciting higher-order beliefs
would test whether households know they revised: under
diagnostic expectations, overreacting households
underestimate how much they changed their forecast,
because the salient signal feels like new information rather
than reweighting. Second, media blackout quasi-experiments
(for example, broadcast interruptions during major
political events) provide exogenous variation in salience
that an IV design could exploit without relying on the weak
geopolitical congestion instrument used here. Third,
within-household revision data at higher frequency than
the biennial CFPS would permit tests for asymmetric
overreaction: diagnostic expectations predict larger
deviations for positive salience shocks (salient inflation
spikes) than for negative ones. Each of these tests would
discriminate diagnostic overreaction from informational
frictions with a precision the current design cannot reach.
