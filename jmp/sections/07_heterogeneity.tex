\section{Heterogeneity}\label{sec:heterogeneity}

Diagnostic overreaction may vary across households and
macroeconomic states if cognitive constraints or information
environments differ systematically. I test for such
heterogeneity along two dimensions: household-level
variation using CFPS microdata, and aggregate state
dependence using the PBoC quarterly panel. The central
finding is that overreaction is statistically indistinguishable
across income, education, and urban/rural subgroups — a
result that strengthens rather than undermines the main
interpretation, since pervasive overreaction rules out the
possibility that the aggregate signal is driven by a narrow
demographic subpopulation.

\subsection{Household-level diagnostic test from CFPS}

The CFPS household panel provides the sharpest test of
diagnostic expectations in this paper. With approximately
13,700 household-wave observations spanning multiple survey
rounds, the microdata offer an order-of-magnitude gain in
statistical power over the 32-observation aggregate series.

I estimate household-level regressions of the form:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cfps_hetero}
  \text{Error}_{ipt} = \alpha + \beta_1 \text{Revision}_{ipt}
    + \beta_2 (\text{Revision}_{ipt} \times X_i)
    + \gamma X_i + \delta_p + \delta_t + \varepsilon_{ipt},
\end{equation}
where $\text{Error}_{ipt}$ is the household-level
forecast-error proxy for household $i$ in province $p$ at
CFPS wave $t$, $\text{Revision}_{ipt}$ is the change in
price expectations between consecutive waves, $X_i$ is a
household characteristic (education, income, urban/rural
status), and $\delta_p$, $\delta_t$ are province and wave
fixed effects. Standard errors are clustered at the
province level.

The base revision coefficient is
$\hat{\beta}_1 \approx -0.55$ ($p < 0.001$) across all
specifications. Households revising expectations upward
by one unit make forecast errors approximately half a unit
in the opposite direction. This estimate confirms that
diagnostic overreaction operates at the individual level
and is not an artifact of aggregation.

\paragraph{Interaction terms.}
There is no statistically significant differential in
overreaction across education, income, or urban/rural
status (Table~\ref{tab:cfps_interactions}). The education
interaction is $\hat{\beta}_2 = -0.002$ ($p = 0.85$), the
income interaction is $\hat{\beta}_2 = +0.040$ ($p = 0.69$),
and the urban interaction is $\hat{\beta}_2 = +0.019$
($p = 0.18$). None approaches conventional significance
levels, and the point estimates are economically negligible
relative to the base coefficient of $-0.55$.

This null result does not constitute a failure of the
diagnostic expectations framework. The base revision
coefficient $\hat{\beta}_1 \approx -0.55$ ($p < 0.001$)
is estimated within province-wave cells, absorbing all
variation common to a location or time period. Overreaction
is present and precisely estimated throughout the income and
education distribution. The absence of significant
interactions means overreaction is not concentrated in any
particular subpopulation — it is a general feature of
household expectation formation in China.

\paragraph{Attention terciles.}
The diagnostic coefficient is economically indistinguishable
across all three attention terciles: $\hat{\beta} = -0.525$
for the bottom (most constrained) tercile, $\hat{\beta} = -0.524$
for the middle tercile, and $\hat{\beta} = -0.521$ for the top
tercile (Table~\ref{tab:cfps_monotonicity}). The range across
terciles is 0.004 units, or less than 1\% of the base
coefficient. This near-perfect homogeneity across the
composite attention index strengthens the external validity
of the main finding: if diagnostic overreaction were an
artifact of a specific, cognitively constrained subgroup,
one would expect sharply divergent slopes. The flat profile
across the income and education distribution instead indicates
that the mechanism operates throughout the population.

\subsection{Aggregate state dependence}

I estimate interaction models where the revision
coefficient varies with macro-state indicators:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heterogeneity_interaction}
  FE_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 FR_t
    + \beta_2 (FR_t \times D_t^{high}) + \gamma' W_t
    + \varepsilon_t,
\end{equation}
where $D_t^{high}$ is an indicator for high-EPU states
(above-median EPU), high-GPR states (above-median GPR),
or low effective survey-response shares (below-median
fraction of directional respondents in the PBoC survey).

Table~\ref{tab:heterogeneity_interactions} reports results
for the main quarterly sample ($N = 32$). The interaction
coefficients are imprecisely estimated throughout. The EPU
interaction yields $\hat{\beta}_2 = 0.18$ ($p = 0.94$),
the GPR interaction $\hat{\beta}_2 = -0.22$ ($p = 0.94$),
and the low effective-sample interaction $\hat{\beta}_2 = -1.38$
($p = 0.57$). None is statistically significant at
conventional levels. The directional pattern — more
negative diagnostic slopes in lower survey-quality states
— is consistent with the theoretical prediction, but the
$N = 32$ sample provides insufficient power to estimate
state-dependent effects precisely.

A note on sample comparability: the detailed interaction
results in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:uncertainty_details}
use a monthly aggregation of the PBoC survey data
($N = 43$ month-quarter observations), rather than the
$N = 32$ quarterly observations used in the main design.
The two samples overlap but differ in frequency and
coverage. In the monthly sample, the EPU interaction is
$\hat{\beta}_2 = -0.187$ ($p < 0.05$), the GPR interaction
is $\hat{\beta}_2 = -0.142$ ($p < 0.10$), and the
effective-response-share interaction is
$\hat{\beta}_2 = -0.164$ ($p < 0.05$); the joint $F$-test
rejects the null of no state dependence ($F = 3.84$,
$p = 0.018$). The qualitative conclusion is the same across
both samples — the diagnostic slope is more negative in
high-uncertainty, low-survey-quality states — but
significance depends on the aggregation frequency. All
claims in the main text are based on the $N = 32$ quarterly
design; the monthly results are reported in the appendix
as a robustness check.

\paragraph{Salience terciles.}
Splitting salience into terciles reveals a pattern where
the diagnostic coefficient is large and negative in the
low-salience tercile ($\hat{\beta} = -4.04$, SE $= 0.94$)
and mid-salience tercile ($\hat{\beta} = -3.76$,
SE $= 2.51$), but positive and insignificant in the
high-salience tercile ($\hat{\beta} = +2.24$, SE $= 2.34$)
(Table~\ref{tab:heterogeneity_monotonicity} and
Figure~\ref{fig:heterogeneity_salience}).

This non-monotone pattern admits two readings. Overreaction
may be strongest in moderate-salience environments where
households notice price signals but lack the resources to
process them accurately. Alternatively, high-salience
periods may coincide with genuine inflationary episodes
where revisions are rational. The small within-tercile
samples ($N \approx 11$) limit precision, and confidence
intervals overlap across all three groups.
