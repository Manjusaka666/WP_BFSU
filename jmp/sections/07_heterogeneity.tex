\section{Heterogeneity and Dynamics}\label{sec:heterogeneity}

This section asks whether the diagnostic pattern documented above is
concentrated in specific households or specific macro states, and
whether it has measurable forward consequences in household panels.
I keep the interpretation conservative throughout. The CFPS
micro-evidence is precise and points to broad pervasiveness of
overreaction. By contrast, the aggregate interaction tests rely on
only 32 quarterly observations and are therefore underpowered for
fine-grained state dependence. The CHFS forward-tracking exercise is
informative but only suggestive.

\subsection{Household Heterogeneity}

The CFPS panel remains the most informative source for heterogeneity
because it has much higher statistical power than the aggregate time
series and lets me absorb province and wave effects directly. I
estimate household-level specifications that interact forecast
revisions with pre-determined household traits. The main coefficient
on revisions is stable across specifications:
$\hat{\beta} = -0.55$ with $p < 0.001$. The magnitude is close to the
baseline CFPS estimate in Section~\ref{sec:results}, indicating that
the interaction design is not mechanically changing the core signal.

The first set of interactions tests whether overreaction is stronger
for less educated, lower-income, or non-urban households. None of
these interaction slopes is statistically different from zero. For
education, the interaction estimate is $-0.002$ ($p = 0.85$). For
income, it is $+0.040$ ($p = 0.69$). For urban status, it is $+0.019$
($p = 0.18$). All three are small relative to the base coefficient of
$-0.55$, and all three have confidence intervals that comfortably span
zero. The empirical message is straightforward: the data do not
support demographic concentration of diagnosticity along these margins.

This pattern matters for interpretation. One concern in aggregate
studies is that negative revision--error slopes could be produced by a
narrow subgroup with unusual beliefs, while the rest of the
population updates approximately rationally. The CFPS interactions do
not support that concern. The base diagnostic slope remains strong and
precise, while the interaction terms are consistently null. That
combination is exactly what one would expect if overreaction is a
general expectation-formation feature rather than a niche behavioral
pathology.

I next split households by an attention index and estimate the same
diagnostic slope within each tercile. The coefficients are tightly
clustered: $-0.525$, $-0.524$, and $-0.521$. The full range is 0.004,
which is less than 1\% of the baseline absolute effect. This near
equality across attention terciles is empirically useful for external
validity. If the mechanism required extreme information constraints,
one would expect a steep gradient across terciles. Instead, the slope
is effectively flat.

Taken together, the CFPS heterogeneity exercises strengthen, rather
than weaken, the baseline interpretation. The evidence does \emph{not}
show that some groups overreact and others do not. It shows a strong
average overreaction effect with no detectable gradient by education,
income, urban status, or attention tercile in this sample. That
pervasiveness makes it less likely that the main result is an artifact
of sample composition.

\subsection{State Dependence}

I now test whether the revision--error slope changes with aggregate
uncertainty and information salience. Conceptually, state dependence
is plausible: diagnostic overreaction may strengthen when salient
signals are noisy, geopolitical tension is high, or attention is
fragmented. Empirically, however, this part of the analysis is limited
by sample size. The quarterly design has $N = 32$, which is enough for
sign checks but not ideal for interaction-heavy inference.

In the EPU and GPR interaction specifications, the interaction terms
are imprecisely estimated and not statistically distinguishable from
zero in the quarterly sample. I therefore treat these regressions as
inconclusive on whether aggregate uncertainty amplifies or attenuates
diagnosticity. The absence of precision here should not be read as
evidence of no state dependence; it is equally consistent with
moderate effects that cannot be pinned down in a short macro panel.

To probe nonlinearity differently, I split the sample by salience
terciles. The tercile-specific coefficients are $-4.04$, $-3.76$, and
$+2.24$. Two features stand out. First, the point estimates are
non-monotone: strongly negative at low and middle salience, then
positive at high salience. Second, the highest-salience estimate
reverses sign but remains imprecise, so the data do not support a
clean threshold story. In practical terms, this pattern is difficult
to map into a single structural channel.

The most cautious reading is that state-dependent dynamics may exist,
but their shape is not identified sharply in the quarterly sample.
One interpretation is that very high salience periods include episodes
where revisions partly reflect genuine inflation news, reducing or
temporarily offsetting diagnostic overreaction. Another is that the
sign change is sampling noise from small within-tercile cell sizes.
Both interpretations are plausible; neither can be ruled out with
precision at $N = 32$.

For that reason, I use the state-dependence results as descriptive
evidence rather than as a central identification pillar. The reliable
fact from this subsection is the lack of monotonicity in the salience
split and the broad imprecision of EPU/GPR interactions in the main
quarterly sample. This motivates retaining the CFPS micro estimates as
the primary quantitative anchor.

\subsection{CHFS Forward-Tracking}

The CFPS evidence shows who overreacts; this subsection asks whether
households identified as overreactors exhibit different medium-run
outcomes in a separate panel. Using CHFS, I classify households in
2011 as overreactors if they are in the top quartile of residualized
inflation expectations. Residualization removes province effects and
standard observables, so the indicator captures within-province
relative overreaction in beliefs rather than mechanical level
differences across locations.

I then track income growth for these households in later waves. The
2011 to 2013 estimate is small and statistically null:
$\hat{\beta} = 0.022$. The 2011 to 2015 estimate is larger,
$\hat{\beta} = 0.106$, and marginally significant at the 10\% level.
The positive sign is not what a simple overspending story would
predict ex ante, where stronger overreaction should map into weaker
subsequent outcomes.

There are at least two reasons to keep interpretation modest. First,
income growth is a noisy proxy for the welfare channel of interest,
which is closer to intertemporal consumption-savings distortions.
Second, the overreactor indicator is based on one baseline wave and a
coarse expectation measure; this introduces classification noise that
can attenuate true effects or distort signs in finite samples. In that
context, a positive and weakly significant long-horizon coefficient is
better viewed as a reduced-form association than as a structural
counterexample to diagnostic behavior.

The forward-tracking result is therefore suggestive, not decisive. The
short-horizon coefficient is null, and the longer-horizon coefficient
is weakly positive with an unexpected sign. Together, these estimates
do not provide strong household-outcome validation for the mechanism,
but they also do not overturn the core evidence from forecast
revision--error dynamics. The CHFS exercise should be read as an
exploratory extension that motivates richer panel measurement in
future work rather than a stand-alone test.
