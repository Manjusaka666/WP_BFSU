\section{Heterogeneity}\label{sec:heterogeneity}

This section examines whether the overreaction pattern varies across
households or macroeconomic states, and whether it has detectable
forward consequences in a separate household panel.

\subsection{Household Heterogeneity}

The CFPS panel offers the most informative test for heterogeneity
because its sample size provides far more statistical power than the
aggregate time series. I interact forecast revisions with
pre-determined household traits. The base coefficient on revisions is
stable across specifications: $\hat{\beta} = -0.55$ with $p < 0.001$,
close to the baseline CFPS estimate in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

None of the interaction terms is statistically different from zero. The
education interaction is $-0.002$ ($p = 0.85$), the income interaction
is $+0.040$ ($p = 0.69$), and the urban status interaction is $+0.019$
($p = 0.18$). All three are small relative to the base coefficient and
have confidence intervals that span zero. The data do not support
demographic concentration of diagnosticity along these margins.

This null pattern is informative. One concern in aggregate studies is
that negative revision--error slopes could be driven by a narrow
subgroup with unusual beliefs while other households update
approximately rationally. The CFPS interactions do not support that
concern. Overreaction appears broad-based across education, income, and
location groups in this sample.

Splitting households by an attention index confirms the same finding.
Tercile-specific coefficients are $-0.525$, $-0.524$, and $-0.521$.
The full range is 0.004, less than 1\% of the baseline absolute
effect. If overreaction required extreme information constraints, one
would expect a steep gradient across terciles. The slope is effectively
flat.

\paragraph{Food-price exposure heterogeneity.}
The strongest papers in this literature tie heterogeneity to the
hypothesized mechanism rather than to generic demographics
\citep{DacuntoHoangPirolliWeber2021}. The CFPS does not contain
province-level food CPI or household-level grocery expenditure data,
so I cannot construct a direct food-price exposure measure. This is a
limitation: the ideal test would interact revisions with province-level
pork or food CPI intensity. The null interaction results on income and
urban status are consistent with broad-based overreaction but do not
speak directly to whether food-price-exposed households overreact more.
Future work with matched food-price data at the province-wave level
could address this gap.

\subsection{State Dependence}

I test whether the revision--error slope changes with aggregate
uncertainty and information salience. This analysis is limited by
sample size: the quarterly design has $N = 32$, which permits sign
checks but not precise interaction estimates.

In the EPU and GPR interaction specifications, the interaction terms
are imprecisely estimated and not statistically distinguishable from
zero. I treat these regressions as inconclusive on whether aggregate
uncertainty amplifies or attenuates diagnosticity. The absence of
precision should not be read as evidence of no state dependence; it is
equally consistent with moderate effects that a short macro panel
cannot detect.

Splitting by salience terciles yields coefficients of $-4.04$,
$-3.76$, and $+2.24$. The pattern is non-monotone: strongly negative
at low and middle salience, then positive at high salience. The
highest-salience estimate reverses sign but remains imprecise, so the
data do not support a clean threshold story. One interpretation is that
high-salience periods include episodes where revisions partly reflect
genuine inflation news. Another is that the sign change is sampling
noise from small within-tercile cell sizes. Neither can be ruled out at
$N = 32$.

I treat the state-dependence results as descriptive. The CFPS micro
estimates remain the primary quantitative anchor.

\subsection{CHFS Forward-Tracking (Exploratory)}

This subsection asks whether households identified as overreactors in
CHFS exhibit different medium-run outcomes. Using the 2011 wave, I
classify households as overreactors if they fall in the top quartile of
residualized inflation expectations after removing province effects and
standard observables. I then track income growth in later waves.

The 2011 to 2013 estimate is small and statistically null:
$\hat{\beta} = 0.022$. The 2011 to 2015 estimate is larger,
$\hat{\beta} = 0.106$, and marginally significant at the 10\% level.
The positive sign does not match what a simple overspending story would
predict, where overreaction should map into weaker subsequent outcomes.

Two factors limit interpretation. First, income growth is a noisy proxy
for the welfare channel of interest, which involves intertemporal
consumption-savings distortions. Second, the overreactor indicator is
based on one wave and a coarse expectation measure, introducing
classification noise that can attenuate true effects or distort signs
in finite samples.

The short-horizon coefficient is null and the longer-horizon
coefficient is weakly positive with an unexpected sign. These estimates
neither confirm nor contradict the overreaction mechanism documented
above. I view this exercise as suggestive evidence that motivates
richer panel measurement in future work rather than a stand-alone test.
