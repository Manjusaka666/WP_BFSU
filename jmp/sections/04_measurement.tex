\section{Measurement}%\label{sec:measurement}

\subsection{Carlson--Parkin quantification}

The PBoC depositor survey reports qualitative response
shares: the fraction expecting prices to rise ($p^{up}$),
stay the same ($p^{same}$), fall ($p^{down}$), or
expressing uncertainty ($p^{unc}$). I convert these into
a quantitative expectation using the \citet{Carlson1975}
method, which assumes individual expected inflation rates
follow a normal distribution. Each respondent reports
``up'' if their expectation exceeds a perception threshold
$\delta$, ``down'' if it falls below $-\delta$, and
``same'' otherwise. Inverting the implied cumulative
normal conditions yields:
\begin{align}
  \mu_t^{CP} &= \delta \cdot
    \frac{\Phi^{-1}(1 - p_t^{up}) +
          \Phi^{-1}(p_t^{down})}
         {\Phi^{-1}(1 - p_t^{up}) -
          \Phi^{-1}(p_t^{down})}, %\label{eq:cp_mean} \\
  \sigma_t^{CP} &= \frac{2\delta}
    {\Phi^{-1}(1 - p_t^{up}) -
     \Phi^{-1}(p_t^{down})}, %\label{eq:cp_sd}
\end{align}
where $\Phi^{-1}$ is the standard normal quantile function
and $\delta$ is the indifference threshold, calibrated to
sample-average realized inflation following standard
practice.

\paragraph{Uncertain respondents.}
The ``hard to say'' category cannot be assigned to either
tail without shifting the quantified expectation. I
construct three measures. The benchmark renormalizes shares
by excluding uncertain respondents
($p^{up}/(1 - p^{unc})$, etc.), distributing them in
proportion to directional shares. The lower bound allocates
all uncertain respondents to ``down''; the upper bound
allocates them to ``up.'' The diagnostic coefficient must
retain its sign across all three for the result to be
credible.

Table~\ref{tab:cp_bounds} reports the expectation series
under each allocation. The bound spread averages roughly
0.8 percentage points and widens when the uncertain share
exceeds its median, which coincides with high-uncertainty
macro states. This covariation between measurement
precision and economic conditions means identification is
hardest where the economic question is most interesting;
the bound analysis makes this tension visible rather than
concealing it behind a point estimate.

\paragraph{Threshold sensitivity.}
The threshold $\delta$ scales the quantified distribution:
higher values stretch $\mu^{CP}$ in absolute terms. I set
$\delta$ equal to sample-average realized CPI inflation
(approximately 0.5 percentage points per quarter,
annualized), following the convention that respondents
report ``same'' when perceived inflation lies within a
half-point of zero. Appendix~\ref{app:data} reports
results under alternative values (0.3, 0.7, 1.0). Because
$\delta$ scales both the revision and the forecast error,
their ratio remains approximately invariant, and the
diagnostic coefficient is stable.

\subsection{CFPS household-level expectations}

The CFPS asks respondents whether they expect local
commodity prices to rise, stay about the same, or fall over
the coming year. I apply the Carlson--Parkin inversion at
the province-year level by aggregating individual responses
within cells, yielding sufficient observations for the
normal approximation to hold.

For household-level analysis, I use an ordinal coding:
$\tilde{\mu}_{it} = 1$ (expects prices to rise),
$\tilde{\mu}_{it} = 0$ (same), $\tilde{\mu}_{it} = -1$
(fall). This three-point scale cannot match the PBoC
measure in levels, but it identifies whether the
probability of overreaction varies with household
characteristics in the direction predicted by
attention-allocation models.

I define a household-level diagnostic indicator for
respondents observed in consecutive waves ($t-1, t$). A
revision is positive if the expectation moved upward (e.g.,
``same'' to ``up''). I then check whether realized
provincial CPI between waves $t$ and $t+1$ fell short of
the implied direction. Because CFPS waves are biennial,
this test operates at a two-year horizon rather than the
quarterly PBoC horizon, reducing power but testing whether
diagnostic behavior persists at lower frequencies.

\subsection{Forecast errors, revisions, and derived objects}

All derived objects follow definitions tied to survey
timing.

\paragraph{Quarterly panel (PBoC).}
The forecast revision is $FR_t = \mu_t^{CP} - \mu_{t-1}^{CP}$,
and the forecast error is $FE_{t+1} = \pi_{t+1}^{QoQ} - \mu_t^{CP}$,
where $\pi_{t+1}^{QoQ}$ is the quarter-on-quarter annualized CPI
inflation rate in the quarter following the survey.

\paragraph{Household panel (CFPS).}
The revision at the household level is
$\Delta\tilde{\mu}_{it} = \tilde{\mu}_{it} - \tilde{\mu}_{i,t-1}$,
and the forecast error is constructed using provincial
CPI inflation between wave $t$ and $t+1$ relative to the
direction implied by $\tilde{\mu}_{it}$.

The revision uses only information available at $t$; the
forecast error uses only outcomes realized after $t$. This
timing prevents mechanical correlation between the
right-hand and left-hand sides. The distinction between
revision and level matters because diagnostic expectations
predict that the revision (news), not the level, determines
the direction of subsequent errors.
Table~\ref{tab:desc_extended} reports the joint
distribution of revisions and errors in the quarterly
panel, confirming that the negative association in
Figure~\ref{fig:revision_error_dynamics} holds across the
full distribution.
