**Referee Report**

**Manuscript:** *Overreaction in Household Inflation Expectations: Evidence from China*
**Recommendation:** Reject

I reviewed the current draft of the manuscript.  JPE Macroeconomics states that it seeks high-quality theoretical and empirical work relevant to macroeconomics, and its data policy requires that the data and empirical implementation be clearly and precisely documented. Against that standard, this version is improved, but I do not think it is yet publishable. ([芝加哥期刊][1])

The paper has moved meaningfully forward. Relative to earlier versions, the draft is more coherent, the treatment construction is now described in a conceptually defensible way using province-level CPI subindices in **previous month = 100** form, the appendix is more tightly aligned with the main text, and the paper now directly acknowledges that the exact quantitative mapping from the ordinal CFPS expectation variable into CPI units is imperfect. The correction of the one-standard-deviation interpretation in the abstract and introduction is also important. These are real improvements. 

That said, I still have substantial reservations. The central issue is no longer that the design is obviously flawed. The problem is that the manuscript still overstates what the design can establish. In its current form, the paper has an interesting and potentially important reduced-form fact, but the mapping from that fact to a strong behavioral conclusion remains too fragile.

My first major concern is the paper’s **headline quantitative comparison with the rational benchmark**. The manuscript repeatedly emphasizes that the estimated forecast-error coefficient is between roughly 121 and 233 times larger than the rational bound. But the benchmark and the empirical object are still not fully commensurate. The theory is written in terms of a one-period signal (s_{pt}), a one-period pass-through relation, and a rational bound based on meat’s CPI weight (\omega). The empirical object, however, is a **cumulative inter-wave meat-price shock** over roughly two years, matched to **forward inter-wave annualized headline CPI**, while persistence is estimated from a **monthly AR(1)** process. Those are not the same time-aggregation objects. The current benchmark is therefore best interpreted as a disciplined heuristic, not as a fully structural quantitative upper bound that can support literal “121×” or “233×” claims. The sign evidence is informative; the exact ratio is not nearly as secure as the paper suggests. 

My second concern is that the paper still presents itself as more micro-identified than it really is. The manuscript reports 90,133 household-wave observations in the expectation equation and 69,533 in the forecast-error equation, but the treatment varies at the **province × wave** level and the pass-through regression is based on only **93 province × wave cells**. The effective identifying variation is therefore low dimensional. The household stack increases the number of residual observations, but it does not change the fact that the identifying signal comes from a small number of treatment cells. This does not invalidate the result, but it materially weakens the evidentiary strength relative to how the paper currently frames it. 

My third concern is that the **mechanism chain is still not hard enough**. The core narrative is that meat shocks raise expectations, do not raise future headline CPI, and therefore generate more negative forecast errors. The pass-through result is helpful. But the expectation channel remains weak in the specification that the manuscript originally emphasized: under region-by-wave fixed effects, the expectation coefficient is positive but imprecise. The new robustness result using **province fixed effects plus region-by-wave fixed effects** is stronger and is an important addition, but that now raises a presentation problem: if this is the most convincing specification, it should play a more central role in the paper rather than being introduced only later as an additional robustness check. At present, the empirical hierarchy and the presentation hierarchy are still not fully aligned. 

My fourth concern is that the **placebo evidence remains suggestive rather than decisive**. The manuscript has improved this section by adding a formal Wald test. That is a useful step. But the key result is still not especially strong. In the expectation equation, equality of the meat and grain coefficients is clearly not rejected. In the forecast-error equation, the difference is only marginally significant at the 10 percent level. That does not mean the placebo fails. It does mean, however, that the paper should present the placebo as supporting but limited evidence, not as a sharp disciplinary test that fully isolates salience as the operative mechanism. The current text still leans too hard on this part of the paper. 

My fifth concern is that **internal consistency has improved, but is still not yet at top-journal standard**. Several examples remain. The text after Table 2 says the forecast-error coefficient is statistically significant across all specifications, but columns (2) and (5) do not in fact carry significance stars. Section 2.3.3 states that the CFPS panel dimension allows control for household fixed effects, but household fixed effects are not used in the reported specifications. Section 3.5 refers to “province-by-wave fixed effects” in describing the preferred design, while the paper’s preferred specification is actually **region-by-wave** fixed effects. In Section 7.2, the discussion of the urban interaction is not fully synchronized with Table 7. Appendix B.3 says forward CPI for the 2022 wave is available in the current extract, while Appendix B.4 says the 2022 wave lacks a complete forward realization and is excluded from the forecast-error sample. None of these points alone is fatal, but collectively they signal that the paper is still not fully under control. 

On references and citation integrity, this draft is clearly better than earlier versions. I no longer view fabricated or obviously spurious references as the main problem. The reference list is now substantially cleaner and more credible. That said, citation discipline is still not where it should be for a top-level submission. Several institutional and quantitative statements in the text, especially those concerning exact pork weights, historical pork contributions to CPI volatility, and some episode-specific institutional details, would benefit from more direct primary-source support rather than broad secondary description. This is no longer the paper’s main weakness, but it is still part of the reason the manuscript does not yet feel publication-ready. 

To be clear, I do think there is a real paper here. The current version has moved from “conceptually troubled” to “potentially publishable somewhere after a substantial further revision.” But the manuscript still needs one more serious round of work before it can plausibly meet a top-field standard. In my view, the highest-priority revisions are fourfold.

First, the rational benchmark needs to be rewritten so that it matches the empirical object in **units and time aggregation**, rather than functioning as a one-period heuristic pasted onto a multi-period empirical design. Second, the strongest fixed-effects specification should be moved into the main empirical core and treated as the paper’s primary specification, not as an after-the-fact reinforcement. Third, the placebo evidence should be presented more cautiously and more transparently, with the forecast-error horse-race results and cross-coefficient tests elevated into the main text. Fourth, the paper needs a final consistency pass to eliminate remaining mismatches in specification descriptions, sample definitions, and interpretation of significance. 

My recommendation is therefore **reject**. The manuscript is better than before, and the central empirical pattern is interesting. But the paper still does not establish its strongest claims with the degree of precision and internal coherence required for publication in its current form.
